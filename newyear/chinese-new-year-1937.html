The Ox holds a special place in the Chinese zodiac, and a Fire Ox year carries its own distinctive energy. Each zodiac year brings a different flavor to the Spring Festival, shaping the hopes and expectations people carry into the months ahead.

Spring Festival Customs in 1937

Chinese New Year customs in 1937 mixed timeless traditions with small, personal touches. Different households emphasized different things, but six practices stood out as central to how the festival was lived that winter. Here is what a typical family's season might have included.

🍭

New Year Sweets and Snacks

Making the holiday snacks was a task shared by the women of the household. Rolling sesame cookies, pressing rice cakes, stirring the sugar syrup that coated candied fruit -- these were the smells and sounds of preparation. In 1937, a jar of the family's favorite sweets sat on every kitchen table.

🧵

Red Envelopes

A red envelope is more than money; it is a transfer of good fortune from one generation to the next. The giver passes on a wish for health, safety, and prosperity. In 1937, this tradition was observed quietly at home after the reunion meal, one child at a time.

👗

Wearing New Clothes

Red was the preferred color for festival wear, but any cheerful color was welcome. Adults tended toward darker, more dignified outfits; children wore whatever made them happy. In 1937, a child in a new red coat walking to a relative's house was the characteristic image of the holiday.

🏯

Temple Visits and Prayers

Some temples had stone statues or sacred trees that visitors touched as they walked past, hoping to receive a little extra luck. Others had large bronze urns where incense sticks were collected, their smoke rising slowly toward the roof. In 1937, these quiet places of worship were full of people moving slowly and respectfully.

🪐

Lion and Dragon Dances

For children, nothing matched the thrill of watching a lion dance. The big-eyed costume, the bobbing head, the loud music -- it all felt like magic. Boys tried to imitate the movements in the courtyard afterward. In 1937, a visiting lion troupe could be the highlight of a child's entire holiday.

🎁

Spring Couplets and Door Gods

Upstairs windows got smaller red decorations, too -- the inverted character for fortune, paper cuttings of the zodiac animal, and simple lucky phrases. The effect was a house that glowed red from the outside. In 1937, preparing all this paper took a full afternoon.

Historical Context of 1937

The world in 1937 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Fire Ox year began.

🌎

China in 1937

China in 1937 was in the midst of profound upheaval. The Second Sino-Japanese War and World War II dominated daily life for millions. Despite the devastation, families still found ways to observe Chinese New Year, often in shelters or displaced communities. The festival became a symbol of endurance -- a reminder that life and tradition would outlast the conflict.

🌐

A World at War

Globally, 1937 fell within the era of World War II. Nations were consumed by conflict, economies were mobilized for war, and ordinary people faced rationing, displacement, and loss. For Chinese communities abroad, celebrating the new year carried an added layer of longing for homeland and hope for peace.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

For ordinary Chinese families in 1937, the Spring Festival was one of the few moments when the hardships of war could be set aside, even briefly. People made do with what they had -- a simple meal, a few firecrackers, a handwritten couplet. The determination to celebrate, no matter how modest, spoke to the deep resilience of Chinese culture.

📚

Tradition as Stability

In times of extreme uncertainty, the rituals of Chinese New Year provided a rare sense of normalcy. The ancestor worship, the reunion dinners, the red decorations -- these practices connected people to their heritage and to each other. They were a lifeline to identity when everything else felt fragile.

A Story from 1937

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Fire Ox year.

Meet De Ming, a doctor in a quiet neighborhood in Shanghai. 1937 started quietly. The weeks before Chinese New Year were colder than usual, and De spent most evenings at home helping a younger sibling practice writing auspicious characters on cheap red paper. The sibling was clumsy at first -- the ink ran, the characters were too large, the paper got wrinkled -- but by the third evening, the work started to look respectable. On New Year's Eve, when the time came to paste the couplets up, the family chose the sibling's best attempt rather than buying professionally written ones. The characters were a little wobbly, the ink was a little uneven, but the words were honest and meant something. Neighbors who walked past the door sometimes glanced twice at the writing, then smiled; they could tell it was a child's work, and they approved. De would later say that this small act -- letting a child write something important -- was what the Fire Ox year meant for the family. The festival, after all, was not about perfection. It was about passing something on.
