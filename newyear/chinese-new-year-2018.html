Chinese New Year 2018 landed on February 16, kicking off the Year of the Earth Dog on the traditional lunisolar calendar. In the 60-year cycle of Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, this was the Wu (戊) year -- the Earth stem paired with the Dog branch.

The Dog holds a special place in the Chinese zodiac, and a Earth Dog year carries its own distinctive energy. Each zodiac year brings a different flavor to the Spring Festival, shaping the hopes and expectations people carry into the months ahead.

Spring Festival Customs in 2018

How did people actually celebrate Chinese New Year in 2018? The customs were deeply rooted in centuries of tradition, with regional variations that made each celebration unique. This year, families focused on six favorite rituals -- each one chosen for its special meaning and its place in the household's routine.

🍭

New Year Sweets and Snacks

Tea was the centerpiece of every holiday visit. A well-made pot of tea, poured into small cups and offered with both hands, was a sign of welcome and respect. In 2018, households bought a little more tea than usual, specifically for the stream of visitors expected in the first week of the new year.

🎓

Reunion Dinner

The reunion dinner was where the year's stories were told. Grandparents recalled festivals from their youth, parents quietly counted the blessings of the past twelve months, and children asked for seconds of everything. In 2018, this meal was the emotional center of the holiday.

🎁

Spring Couplets and Door Gods

Upstairs windows got smaller red decorations, too -- the inverted character for fortune, paper cuttings of the zodiac animal, and simple lucky phrases. The effect was a house that glowed red from the outside. In 2018, preparing all this paper took a full afternoon.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

In the week before the new year, markets overflowed. Housewives bought special ingredients, children were measured for new clothes, and every household stocked up on candies and nuts for visiting relatives. In 2018, the busiest market days felt almost crowded enough to be festive in themselves.

🧵

Red Envelopes

The handing out of red envelopes was the highlight of many a childhood. Married couples gave to the young, and the youngest often received the most. Inside each packet lay a small sum -- not enough to spend recklessly, but enough to feel seen and blessed. In 2018, the envelopes were often homemade, folded from plain red paper.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

Spring cleaning was not just about dirt. Windows were washed, old newspapers were cleared, worn-out clothes were mended or replaced, and kitchens were scrubbed. The idea was to start the new year with an uncluttered home. In 2018, the cleaning was done a day or two before the festival so that the brooms could be put away before New Year's Day -- sweeping on the holiday itself was considered bad luck.

Core Meaning

The Earth Dog year carries a unique blend of symbolism -- renewal, vitality, and the promise of fresh starts. Chinese New Year in 2018, the Year of the Earth Dog, was celebrated with hopes that reflected the zodiac animal's traditional character and the element's particular energy. Each year in the Chinese lunisolar calendar has its own meaning. The Earth Dog year beginning in 2018 brought expectations shaped by centuries of tradition. For families celebrating in 2018, the Earth Dog year carried a specific set of wishes: for health, for prosperity, for safety, and for the quiet happiness of a home in order.

🌿

Good Fortune

Prosperity is not only about money. In the Chinese tradition, a prosperous year is one where the family stays healthy, relationships stay warm, and there is enough food on the table. The Dog year in 2018 was celebrated with these hopes in mind.

🔴

A Fresh Start

Each new lunar year offers a chance to begin again. In 2018, under the sign of the Dog, families saw the festival as an invitation to reset their habits, their homes, and their hopes.

🌿

The Dog's Character

The Dog has long been associated with loyalty, honesty, and a sincere commitment to the people and principles one cares about. Celebrating the festival in this year was a way of recognizing those qualities and hoping to see more of them in the months ahead.

🏙

Wisdom of the Earth Element

The Earth element shapes the character of the year. Earth years are steady, practical, and good for saving, building, and long-term planning.

Historical Context of 2018

The world in 2018 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Earth Dog year began.

🌎

China in 2018

China in 2018 was a global superpower with the world's second-largest economy. Technology was transforming daily life -- smartphones, mobile payments, and e-commerce were reshaping how people celebrated Chinese New Year. Digital red envelopes sent via WeChat became a new tradition alongside ancient customs.

🌐

The Digital Age

The 2010s brought unprecedented technological change to China. For Chinese New Year in 2018, this meant that families separated by distance could connect via video calls, red envelopes could be sent with a tap, and the Spring Festival Gala was streamed on multiple platforms. The festival was adapting to the digital era.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

Life in 2018 China was fast-paced and digitally connected. Yet during Chinese New Year, people still made the journey home. The Chunyun travel rush reached record numbers, with billions of trips made by rail, road, and air. The desire to be with family during the festival was stronger than ever.

📚

Tradition as Stability

Even as technology transformed every aspect of life, Chinese New Year in 2018 proved that some things don't change. The reunion dinner, the ancestor worship, the wish for good fortune -- these rituals connected the digital generation to their grandparents' generation.

A Story from 2018

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Earth Dog year.

Meet Jin Hua, a schoolteacher in a rural community in Shandong. 2018 started quietly. The weeks before Chinese New Year were colder than usual, and Jin spent most evenings at home helping a younger sibling practice writing auspicious characters on cheap red paper. The sibling was clumsy at first -- the ink ran, the characters were too large, the paper got wrinkled -- but by the third evening, the work started to look respectable. On New Year's Eve, when the time came to paste the couplets up, the family chose the sibling's best attempt rather than buying professionally written ones. The characters were a little wobbly, the ink was a little uneven, but the words were honest and meant something. Neighbors who walked past the door sometimes glanced twice at the writing, then smiled; they could tell it was a child's work, and they approved. Jin would later say that this small act -- letting a child write something important -- was what the Earth Dog year meant for the family. The festival, after all, was not about perfection. It was about passing something on.
