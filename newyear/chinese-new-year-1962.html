Spring Festival Customs in 1962

How did people actually celebrate Chinese New Year in 1962? The customs were deeply rooted in centuries of tradition, with regional variations that made each celebration unique. This year, families focused on six favorite rituals -- each one chosen for its special meaning and its place in the household's routine.

🎓

Reunion Dinner

The reunion dinner was where the year's stories were told. Grandparents recalled festivals from their youth, parents quietly counted the blessings of the past twelve months, and children asked for seconds of everything. In 1962, this meal was the emotional center of the holiday.

🧵

Red Envelopes

Children waited most eagerly for the hongbao. Grandparents slipped them quietly into waiting palms, always with a soft blessing and a reminder to study hard. The red paper itself was considered lucky; the money inside was secondary. In 1962, a few coins could make a child's whole holiday.

🍭

New Year Sweets and Snacks

The 'eight treasures' box was a holiday favorite. Eight small compartments held eight different treats, each chosen for its auspicious name or shape. Guests were invited to help themselves, and children were sometimes allowed a second helping if they had been well behaved. In 1962, such boxes were often hand-painted and passed down through families.

🎁

Spring Couplets and Door Gods

Families pasted red paper couplets with auspicious phrases on their doorways, along with images of door gods to protect the household. Writing or buying new couplets before the new year was an essential preparation, and the best calligraphers in the village were always in high demand in 1962.

🏯

Temple Visits and Prayers

Temple visits were not only about asking for things. They were also about thankfulness -- for a safe year past, for the health of family members, for enough to eat. Families lit three sticks of incense each, bowed before the main altar, and sometimes left small offerings of fruit or oil. In 1962, the larger temples held special ceremonies throughout the first week.

👩

Visiting Relatives and Friends

The first words spoken on New Year's Day mattered. Traditional greetings were exchanged -- wishes for health, prosperity, safe children, and successful work. Younger generations knelt or bowed to their elders; elders gave blessings and often a small red envelope. In 1962, these formal gestures were still followed with real care.

Historical Context of 1962

The world in 1962 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Water Tiger year began.

🌎

China in 1962

China in 1962 was recovering from the Great Leap Forward and the devastating famine that followed. The country was in a period of economic adjustment and political recalibration. Chinese New Year offered a moment of relief and reflection for families who had endured years of hardship.

🌐

Recovery and Rebuilding

The early to mid-1960s were a time of cautious recovery for China. The government scaled back some of the more extreme policies of the previous decade, and there was a sense of gradual stabilization. For ordinary people, the Spring Festival was a chance to come together and look forward with cautious optimism.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

Families celebrating Chinese New Year in 1962 were still feeling the effects of the famine years. Meals may have been simpler than in the past, but the spirit of the festival remained strong. People understood that being together was what mattered most.

📚

Tradition as Stability

In the aftermath of one of the most difficult periods in modern Chinese history, the rituals of Chinese New Year took on renewed significance. They were a reminder that culture and community could survive even the harshest conditions.

A Story from 1962

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Water Tiger year.

Meet Li Wang, a store owner in a farming village in Sichuan. 1962 started quietly. The weeks before Chinese New Year were colder than usual, and Li spent most evenings at home helping a younger sibling practice writing auspicious characters on cheap red paper. The sibling was clumsy at first -- the ink ran, the characters were too large, the paper got wrinkled -- but by the third evening, the work started to look respectable. On New Year's Eve, when the time came to paste the couplets up, the family chose the sibling's best attempt rather than buying professionally written ones. The characters were a little wobbly, the ink was a little uneven, but the words were honest and meant something. Neighbors who walked past the door sometimes glanced twice at the writing, then smiled; they could tell it was a child's work, and they approved. Li would later say that this small act -- letting a child write something important -- was what the Water Tiger year meant for the family. The festival, after all, was not about perfection. It was about passing something on.
