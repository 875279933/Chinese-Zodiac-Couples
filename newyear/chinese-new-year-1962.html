In the 60-year cycle of Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, this was the Ren (壬) year, the Ren stem paired with the Yin (寅) branch. The Tiger holds a set place in the Chinese zodiac, and a Water Tiger year carries its own tone. Spring Festival Customs in 1962

The 1962 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Water Tiger year.

01

Visiting Relatives

The first days of the year were for rounds of visits. Married couples called on the wife's family on the second day. In 1962, the route was planned days ahead, and every stop came with tea, snacks, and good wishes.

02

Lantern Night

On the fifteenth night, lanterns went up. Children carried rabbits and fish made of paper and wire, candlelit from inside. In 1962, the festival closed the way it opened, with light.

03

Spring Couplets

Red paper strips went up on every door, black ink brushed in pairs, with a shorter line across the top. Door gods stood guard at the entrance. In 1962, a good calligrapher could fill half a street with orders in the days before the festival.

04

Lion and Dragon Dances

A lion dance troupe needed two strong dancers and a drummer. A dragon dance needed dozens, each holding a pole that held up the long body. In 1962, such performances drew the biggest crowds of the fair.

05

Ancestor Offerings

Before the meal, families set places for those who came before. Incense, a cup of wine, a bow. In 1962, the ancestor altar was the quiet center of the loudest week of the year.

06

Spring Cleaning

A few days before the new year, every household swept, scrubbed, and mended. Windows came clean, old papers went out, and worn clothes got patched. The point was to step into the new year unburdened. In 1962, brooms went back in the corner before New Year's Day, since sweeping on the holiday itself was said to sweep the luck out.

Cultural Context of 1962

The 1962 festival opened the Water Tiger year against the backdrop of its time. Here is a look at the cultural and social life families carried into the festival.

Cultural Snapshot: 1956-1965

Shared Stoves

In many neighborhoods, families cooked on shared stoves set up in the lane. The reunion meal came together dish by dish as each household contributed something, and the smell of braised meat drifted through the whole street.

Shift Holidays

Factories and work units arranged time off in shifts so that some families ate on the eve and others on the first day. The exact hour mattered less than the fact that the meal happened at all.

Handmade Lanterns

Store-bought lanterns were hard to find, so fathers and grandfathers built them from bamboo and paper. A child in 1962 carried a lantern shaped like a rabbit that had taken three evenings to make.

Radio Evenings

With a radio in the room, the family could hear opera and storytelling while they folded dumplings. The set was small and the signal came and went, but it gave the evening a soundtrack.

A Story from 1962

The meaning of the 1962 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Water Tiger year.

Meet Rong Hua, a potter living in a farming village in Sichuan. 1962 was supposed to be an ordinary Chinese New Year, but it became one Rong Hua would not forget. It started with a small disaster: the kitchen stove broke on New Year's Eve morning, right when the reunion meal was meant to be cooked. The house went quiet. Then neighbors stepped in. One lent a portable stove, another sent a pot of steaming rice, and a shopkeeper kept her door open an extra hour so the family could buy a replacement. By late afternoon the kitchen worked again, and by evening the table was full. The meal that year tasted better, maybe because it almost did not happen. Rong Hua took a simple lesson from the Water Tiger year: good fortune often looks like neighbors looking out for each other. After midnight, with firecrackers rolling down the street, Rong Hua wrote a line in a notebook: remember the stove.
