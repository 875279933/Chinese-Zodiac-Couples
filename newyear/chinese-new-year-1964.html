Spring Festival Customs in 1964

The 1964 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Wood Dragon year.

01

New Year Shopping

In the days before the festival, the market ran late. Stalls of red paper, dried fruit, fireworks, and cloth crowded the lanes. In 1964, shoppers carried bundles home through streets strung with lanterns.

02

Reunion Dinner

The night before the new year, families gathered around one table. Fish for surplus, dumplings for wealth, long noodles for long life, a chicken left whole to keep the family whole. In 1964, this was the meal people traveled all year to reach.

03

Red Envelopes

Married adults tucked crisp bills into red envelopes and pressed them into children's hands. The amount mattered less than the gesture. In 1964, a child might carry a stack of hongbao home and count them late into the night.

04

New Clothes

Red was the favorite, but any bright color would do. Children wore what made them happy; adults leaned toward darker, quieter tones. In 1964, a child in a new red coat walking to a relative's house was the picture of the holiday.

05

Lantern Night

On the fifteenth night, lanterns went up. Children carried rabbits and fish made of paper and wire, candlelit from inside. In 1964, the festival closed the way it opened, with light.

06

Spring Couplets

Red paper strips went up on every door, black ink brushed in pairs, with a shorter line across the top. Door gods stood guard at the entrance. In 1964, a good calligrapher could fill half a street with orders in the days before the festival.

Cultural Context of 1964

The 1964 festival opened the Wood Dragon year against the backdrop of its time. Here is a look at the cultural and social life families carried into the festival.

Cultural Snapshot: 1956-1965

Shared Stoves

In many neighborhoods, families cooked on shared stoves set up in the lane. The reunion meal came together dish by dish as each household contributed something, and the smell of braised meat drifted through the whole street.

Shift Holidays

Factories and work units arranged time off in shifts so that some families ate on the eve and others on the first day. The exact hour mattered less than the fact that the meal happened at all.

Handmade Lanterns

Store-bought lanterns were hard to find, so fathers and grandfathers built them from bamboo and paper. A child in 1964 carried a lantern shaped like a rabbit that had taken three evenings to make.

Radio Evenings

With a radio in the room, the family could hear opera and storytelling while they folded dumplings. The set was small and the signal came and went, but it gave the evening a soundtrack.

A Story from 1964

The meaning of the 1964 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Wood Dragon year.

Meet Yan Ling, a bicycle repairman living in a coastal neighborhood in Guangzhou. 1964 was the year Yan Ling almost did not make it home. The train was late, the platform was crowded, and the journey that should have taken hours stretched into a full day. Yan Ling dozed standing up, woke to snack sellers pushing through the car, and watched the winter fields pass in the dark. When the train finally pulled in, the streets were already lit with red lanterns and the air smelled like firecrackers. Yan Ling reached the family door just as the reunion meal was being set out. There was no time to change, no time to rest. Yan Ling sat down still wearing the coat from the road, and the first bite of the meal was the best Yan Ling had tasted all year. The Wood Dragon year began with a simple reminder: the hardest part of the holiday is getting there, and the rest takes care of itself.
