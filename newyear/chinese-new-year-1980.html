Chinese New Year 1980 landed on February 16, kicking off the Year of the Metal Monkey on the traditional lunisolar calendar. In the 60-year cycle of Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, this was the Geng (庚) year -- the Metal stem paired with the Monkey branch.

Spring Festival Customs in 1980

In 1980, as always, Spring Festival was shaped by the rituals people chose. From food to decoration to ceremony, each custom connected households to a shared heritage. Here are six of the most important customs observed during the Metal Monkey year.

🏯

Temple Visits and Prayers

Temple visits were not only about asking for things. They were also about thankfulness -- for a safe year past, for the health of family members, for enough to eat. Families lit three sticks of incense each, bowed before the main altar, and sometimes left small offerings of fruit or oil. In 1980, the larger temples held special ceremonies throughout the first week.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

Spring cleaning was not just about dirt. Windows were washed, old newspapers were cleared, worn-out clothes were mended or replaced, and kitchens were scrubbed. The idea was to start the new year with an uncluttered home. In 1980, the cleaning was done a day or two before the festival so that the brooms could be put away before New Year's Day -- sweeping on the holiday itself was considered bad luck.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

In the week before the new year, markets overflowed. Housewives bought special ingredients, children were measured for new clothes, and every household stocked up on candies and nuts for visiting relatives. In 1980, the busiest market days felt almost crowded enough to be festive in themselves.

🙏

Ancestor Worship

Ancestor worship was not a grand spectacle; it was the small things done consistently. A bowl of rice on the altar, a few sticks of incense left to burn down, paper spirit money folded carefully and set aside for burning. In 1980, these daily acts were what kept the connection to past generations alive.

🪐

Lion and Dragon Dances

For children, nothing matched the thrill of watching a lion dance. The big-eyed costume, the bobbing head, the loud music -- it all felt like magic. Boys tried to imitate the movements in the courtyard afterward. In 1980, a visiting lion troupe could be the highlight of a child's entire holiday.

👗

Wearing New Clothes

Red was the preferred color for festival wear, but any cheerful color was welcome. Adults tended toward darker, more dignified outfits; children wore whatever made them happy. In 1980, a child in a new red coat walking to a relative's house was the characteristic image of the holiday.

Prosperity and Abundance

In Chinese culture, the Monkey represents prosperity through cleverness -- finding opportunity where others see only problems. Celebrating the Metal Monkey's arrival was an invitation to welcome abundance into the home for the year ahead.

Historical Context of 1980

The world in 1980 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Metal Monkey year began.

🌎

China in 1980

China in 1980 was emerging from the Cultural Revolution and entering a new era under Deng Xiaoping's reform policies. The country was opening up, and traditional customs like Chinese New Year were being revived after years of suppression. For the first time in a decade, people could celebrate openly and with genuine enthusiasm.

🌐

Reform and Opening Up

The late 1970s marked a turning point for China. Deng Xiaoping's reforms began to transform the economy and society. Traditional festivals, including Chinese New Year, were no longer discouraged. Instead, they were embraced as part of China's cultural heritage. The Spring Festival of 1980 was a celebration not just of the new year, but of freedom.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

After years of restraint, families in 1980 celebrated Chinese New Year with renewed energy. Markets filled with holiday goods, temples reopened, and lion dances returned to the streets. The festival became a symbol of the new era -- vibrant, hopeful, and unapologetically Chinese.

📚

Tradition as Stability

The revival of Chinese New Year in the late 1970s showed how deeply embedded the festival is in Chinese life. It wasn't just a holiday -- it was an expression of identity. After the Cultural Revolution, reclaiming these traditions was a way for people to reconnect with their heritage.

Meet Mei Zhen, an assistant in a busy district in Beijing. The week before the festival, the market was busier than usual. Mei came home with bags of rice, a string of firecrackers wrapped in brown paper, and a small red envelope the shopkeeper slipped to her with a wink. On New Year's Eve, the house was full. The stove stayed hot from morning until late at night, the siblings ran errands between the kitchen and the dining table, and the grandmother sat by the window folding paper envelopes while telling stories of festivals from her youth. At midnight, Mei lit the longest string of firecrackers the family could afford, and the whole neighborhood watched the sparks climb into the dark sky. Years later, Mei would remember 1980 not for anything dramatic, but for the feeling of the family gathered in one room -- steam from the hot dishes curling against the ceiling, red decorations glowing on every wall, and the quiet certainty that whatever came next, they would face it together.
