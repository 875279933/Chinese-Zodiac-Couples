Spring Festival Customs in 1948

In 1948, as always, Spring Festival was shaped by the rituals people chose. From food to decoration to ceremony, each custom connected households to a shared heritage. Here are six of the most important customs observed during the Earth Rat year.

🏯

Temple Visits and Prayers

Some temples had stone statues or sacred trees that visitors touched as they walked past, hoping to receive a little extra luck. Others had large bronze urns where incense sticks were collected, their smoke rising slowly toward the roof. In 1948, these quiet places of worship were full of people moving slowly and respectfully.

🍭

New Year Sweets and Snacks

Making the holiday snacks was a task shared by the women of the household. Rolling sesame cookies, pressing rice cakes, stirring the sugar syrup that coated candied fruit -- these were the smells and sounds of preparation. In 1948, a jar of the family's favorite sweets sat on every kitchen table.

🙏

Ancestor Worship

Paying respects to ancestors was a solemn and essential part of Chinese New Year. Families set up altars with food offerings, incense, and paper money to honor those who had passed. This ritual connected the living with their heritage and reinforced the importance of family continuity in 1948.

🎓

Reunion Dinner

Nothing mattered more on New Year's Eve than sitting down together as a family. The table filled up slowly -- a whole steamed fish at one end, dumplings folded that morning at the other, a golden nian gao waiting for dessert. In 1948, mothers spent all day in the kitchen, and children were told to eat every last bite.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

Special foods dominated the last week of preparation. Glutinous rice cakes were steamed, dried tofu was pressed, and candied fruits were jarred. Each item had its own symbolic value, from longevity to prosperity. In 1948, the kitchen was never busier than on the day before New Year's Eve.

🧵

Red Envelopes

The handing out of red envelopes was the highlight of many a childhood. Married couples gave to the young, and the youngest often received the most. Inside each packet lay a small sum -- not enough to spend recklessly, but enough to feel seen and blessed. In 1948, the envelopes were often homemade, folded from plain red paper.

Historical Context of 1948

The world in 1948 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Earth Rat year began.

🌎

China in 1948

China in 1948 was navigating the turbulent aftermath of World War II and the intensifying Chinese Civil War. Society was fractured, the economy was in shambles, and millions were displaced. Yet Chinese New Year remained a constant -- a festival that transcended political divisions and brought families together, however briefly.

🌐

A Nation in Transition

The late 1940s were a period of dramatic change for China. The struggle between the Nationalists and Communists shaped every aspect of life. For ordinary people, the new year was less about politics and more about survival, hope, and the enduring belief that things would eventually get better.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

Despite the chaos, Chinese families in 1948 still observed the Spring Festival. Markets may have been sparse, and travel dangerous, but people found ways to honor the occasion. A shared meal, a prayer for the ancestors, a wish for peace -- these small acts carried enormous weight.

📚

Tradition as Stability

When institutions fail and governments change, tradition endures. Chinese New Year in 1948 was proof of that. The festival gave people something to hold onto when the ground beneath them was shifting. It was a reminder that some things -- family, community, cultural identity -- are bigger than any political moment.

A Story from 1948

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Earth Rat year.

Meet Chun Yan, a store owner born in a quiet neighborhood in Shanghai. 1948 was supposed to be an ordinary Chinese New Year, but it turned into one of the holidays Chun would remember forever. It started with a small disaster: the stove in the family kitchen broke on New Year's Eve morning, right before the holiday meal was supposed to be cooked. The house felt suddenly, unexpectedly quiet. But neighbors rallied. One lent a portable stove; another sent over a large pot of already-steaming rice. A nearby shopkeeper kept her doors open a little longer just so the family could buy a replacement. By mid-afternoon, the kitchen was working again, if a little awkwardly, and by evening the dining table was full of dishes after all. The meal that year tasted better than usual, perhaps because it had nearly not happened. Chun learned something simple about the Earth Rat year: that good fortune sometimes looks like ordinary people helping each other out. After midnight, as the firecrackers went off up and down the street, Chun wrote a short note in a notebook -- 'Remember the stove.'
