Spring Festival Customs in 1960

The 1960 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Metal Rat year.

🏯

Temple Visits

On the first morning, families walked to the temple with incense. The early arrivals hoped to be first to the altar. In 1960, temple courtyards filled with new clothes and low murmurs before sunrise.

🔥

Ancestor Offerings

Before the meal, families set places for those who came before. Incense, a cup of wine, a bow. In 1960, the ancestor altar was the quiet center of the loudest week of the year.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

A few days before the new year, every household swept, scrubbed, and mended. Windows came clean, old papers went out, and worn clothes got patched. The point was to step into the new year unburdened. In 1960, brooms went back in the corner before New Year's Day, since sweeping on the holiday itself was said to sweep the luck out.

🎇

Firecrackers

At midnight the sky lit up. Long strings of firecrackers unrolled from rooftops, and children clapped hands over their ears. The noise chased the old year off. In 1960, the smoke hung low over the rooftops and mixed with the smell of cooking oil.

👗

New Clothes

Red was the favorite, but any bright color would do. Children wore what made them happy; adults leaned toward darker, quieter tones. In 1960, a child in a new red coat walking to a relative's house was the picture of the holiday.

🛍

New Year Shopping

In the days before the festival, the market ran late. Stalls of red paper, dried fruit, fireworks, and cloth crowded the lanes. In 1960, shoppers carried bundles home through streets strung with lanterns.

Cultural Context of 1960

The 1960 festival opened the Metal Rat year against the backdrop of its time. Here is a look at the cultural and social life families carried into the festival.

Cultural Snapshot: 1956-1965
🍲

Shared Stoves

In many neighborhoods, families cooked on shared stoves set up in the lane. The reunion meal came together dish by dish as each household contributed something, and the smell of braised meat drifted through the whole street.

📟

Shift Holidays

Factories and work units arranged time off in shifts so that some families ate on the eve and others on the first day. The exact hour mattered less than the fact that the meal happened at all.

🎈

Handmade Lanterns

Store-bought lanterns were hard to find, so fathers and grandfathers built them from bamboo and paper. A child in 1960 carried a lantern shaped like a rabbit that had taken three evenings to make.

📺

Radio Evenings

With a radio in the room, the family could hear opera and storytelling while they folded dumplings. The set was small and the signal came and went, but it gave the evening a soundtrack.

A Story from 1960

The meaning of the 1960 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Metal Rat year.

Bo Chen, shop clerk, a tea-growing village in Anhui.

Meet Bo Chen, a shop clerk living in a tea-growing village in Anhui. 1960 was the year Bo Chen finally understood what Chinese New Year was for. Until then, Bo Chen had cared mostly about new clothes, red envelopes, and a few days off. But that winter, the elder relative who usually hosted the reunion dinner fell ill. Without a host, the family briefly wondered whether the dinner would happen at all. Then, quietly, people stepped up. An aunt offered her kitchen. An uncle took over the shopping. A cousin, normally shy, found an old calligraphy set and wrote the spring couplets. By New Year's Eve the family was gathered around a different table in a different room, but everything important was the same. The dishes carried their old meanings, the altar was set with care, and the room stayed warm. For Bo Chen, the Metal Rat year was a lesson: tradition lives in people, not in places.
