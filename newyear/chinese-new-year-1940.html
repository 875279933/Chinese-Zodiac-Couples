Chinese New Year 1940 landed on February 8, kicking off the Year of the Metal Dragon on the traditional lunisolar calendar. In the 60-year cycle of Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, this was the Geng (庚) year -- the Metal stem paired with the Dragon branch.

The Dragon holds a special place in the Chinese zodiac, and a Metal Dragon year carries its own distinctive energy. Each zodiac year brings a different flavor to the Spring Festival, shaping the hopes and expectations people carry into the months ahead.

Spring Festival Customs in 1940

How did people actually celebrate Chinese New Year in 1940? The customs were deeply rooted in centuries of tradition, with regional variations that made each celebration unique. This year, families focused on six favorite rituals -- each one chosen for its special meaning and its place in the household's routine.

🏯

Temple Visits and Prayers

Some temples had stone statues or sacred trees that visitors touched as they walked past, hoping to receive a little extra luck. Others had large bronze urns where incense sticks were collected, their smoke rising slowly toward the roof. In 1940, these quiet places of worship were full of people moving slowly and respectfully.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

In the week before the new year, markets overflowed. Housewives bought special ingredients, children were measured for new clothes, and every household stocked up on candies and nuts for visiting relatives. In 1940, the busiest market days felt almost crowded enough to be festive in themselves.

👗

Wearing New Clothes

Some families set aside a special item each year -- a silk scarf for the daughter, a woolen vest for the son, a sturdy pair of shoes for the father. These items were worn on festival days and stored carefully for the rest of the year. In 1940, such pieces were sometimes the only new clothing a family bought.

🧵

Red Envelopes

The handing out of red envelopes was the highlight of many a childhood. Married couples gave to the young, and the youngest often received the most. Inside each packet lay a small sum -- not enough to spend recklessly, but enough to feel seen and blessed. In 1940, the envelopes were often homemade, folded from plain red paper.

🎓

Reunion Dinner

Nothing mattered more on New Year's Eve than sitting down together as a family. The table filled up slowly -- a whole steamed fish at one end, dumplings folded that morning at the other, a golden nian gao waiting for dessert. In 1940, mothers spent all day in the kitchen, and children were told to eat every last bite.

👩

Visiting Relatives and Friends

On the first few days of the new year, people visited relatives and friends, exchanging greetings and good wishes. Married couples visited the wife's family on the second day, a tradition that brought households together in a warm, orderly flow. In 1940, social calls were carefully planned days in advance.

Core Meaning

The Metal Dragon year carries a unique blend of symbolism -- renewal, vitality, and the promise of fresh starts. Chinese New Year in 1940, the Year of the Metal Dragon, was celebrated with hopes that reflected the zodiac animal's traditional character and the element's particular energy. Each year in the Chinese lunisolar calendar has its own meaning. The Metal Dragon year beginning in 1940 brought expectations shaped by centuries of tradition. For families celebrating in 1940, the Metal Dragon year carried a specific set of wishes: for health, for prosperity, for safety, and for the quiet happiness of a home in order.

The Year's Energy

Every combination of animal and element has its own character. Metal years reward discipline, sharp thinking, structured effort, and careful planning.

🌿

The Dragon's Character

The Dragon has long been associated with power, authority, and the kind of grand good fortune people dream of. Celebrating the festival in this year was a way of recognizing those qualities and hoping to see more of them in the months ahead.

🌿

Traditional Blessings

Abundance in Chinese tradition is shown through symbolism. A fish on the dinner table stands for having more than enough. Dumplings stand for wealth. Tangerines stand for good fortune. In 1940, these small symbols were everywhere.

🔴

A Fresh Start

Each new lunar year offers a chance to begin again. In 1940, under the sign of the Dragon, families saw the festival as an invitation to reset their habits, their homes, and their hopes.

Historical Context of 1940

The world in 1940 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Metal Dragon year began.

🌎

China in 1940

China in 1940 was in the midst of profound upheaval. The Second Sino-Japanese War and World War II dominated daily life for millions. Despite the devastation, families still found ways to observe Chinese New Year, often in shelters or displaced communities. The festival became a symbol of endurance -- a reminder that life and tradition would outlast the conflict.

🌐

A World at War

Globally, 1940 fell within the era of World War II. Nations were consumed by conflict, economies were mobilized for war, and ordinary people faced rationing, displacement, and loss. For Chinese communities abroad, celebrating the new year carried an added layer of longing for homeland and hope for peace.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

For ordinary Chinese families in 1940, the Spring Festival was one of the few moments when the hardships of war could be set aside, even briefly. People made do with what they had -- a simple meal, a few firecrackers, a handwritten couplet. The determination to celebrate, no matter how modest, spoke to the deep resilience of Chinese culture.

📚

Tradition as Stability

In times of extreme uncertainty, the rituals of Chinese New Year provided a rare sense of normalcy. The ancestor worship, the reunion dinners, the red decorations -- these practices connected people to their heritage and to each other. They were a lifeline to identity when everything else felt fragile.

A Story from 1940

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Metal Dragon year.

Meet Ah Chen, a woodcutter in a coastal town in Fujian. 1940 started quietly. The weeks before Chinese New Year were colder than usual, and Ah spent most evenings at home helping a younger sibling practice writing auspicious characters on cheap red paper. The sibling was clumsy at first -- the ink ran, the characters were too large, the paper got wrinkled -- but by the third evening, the work started to look respectable. On New Year's Eve, when the time came to paste the couplets up, the family chose the sibling's best attempt rather than buying professionally written ones. The characters were a little wobbly, the ink was a little uneven, but the words were honest and meant something. Neighbors who walked past the door sometimes glanced twice at the writing, then smiled; they could tell it was a child's work, and they approved. Ah would later say that this small act -- letting a child write something important -- was what the Metal Dragon year meant for the family. The festival, after all, was not about perfection. It was about passing something on.
