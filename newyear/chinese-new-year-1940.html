Chinese New Year 1940 landed on February 8, 1940, opening the Year of the Metal Dragon on the traditional lunisolar calendar. In the 60-year cycle of Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, this was the Geng (庚) year, the Geng stem paired with the Chen (辰) branch.

Spring Festival Customs in 1940

The 1940 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Metal Dragon year.

👪

Visiting Relatives

The first days of the year were for rounds of visits. Married couples called on the wife's family on the second day. In 1940, the route was planned days ahead, and every stop came with tea, snacks, and good wishes.

🎈

Lantern Night

On the fifteenth night, lanterns went up. Children carried rabbits and fish made of paper and wire, candlelit from inside. In 1940, the festival closed the way it opened, with light.

🎁

Spring Couplets

Red paper strips went up on every door, black ink brushed in pairs, with a shorter line across the top. Door gods stood guard at the entrance. In 1940, a good calligrapher could fill half a street with orders in the days before the festival.

🪐

Lion and Dragon Dances

A lion dance troupe needed two strong dancers and a drummer. A dragon dance needed dozens, each holding a pole that held up the long body. In 1940, such performances drew the biggest crowds of the fair.

🔥

Ancestor Offerings

Before the meal, families set places for those who came before. Incense, a cup of wine, a bow. In 1940, the ancestor altar was the quiet center of the loudest week of the year.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

A few days before the new year, every household swept, scrubbed, and mended. Windows came clean, old papers went out, and worn clothes got patched. The point was to step into the new year unburdened. In 1940, brooms went back in the corner before New Year's Day, since sweeping on the holiday itself was said to sweep the luck out.

Symbols on the Table

Abundance in Chinese tradition shows up through symbols. A fish on the table stands for having more than enough. Dumplings stand for wealth. Tangerines stand for good fortune. In 1940, these small signs were everywhere.

Home and Heritage

At its center, Chinese New Year is about coming together. The festival gives families a chance to honor the people who came before, mend the ties that frayed, and reset the household before the year turns.

Cultural Context of 1940

The 1940 festival opened the Metal Dragon year against the backdrop of its time. Here is a look at the cultural and social life families carried into the festival.

Cultural Snapshot: 1936-1945

Temple Fairs

The temple fair was the social event of the season. Stalls sold sugar figures, dried fruit, and paper toys, while opera stages drew crowds from early morning. In 1940, the fair was where a child spent their first red-envelope money.

Market Life

In the week before the new year, the market ran late into the evening. Shoppers bargained over cloth, fish, and fireworks while lanterns swung overhead. The lanes stayed loud until the last family had its supplies home.

Homemade Decorations

Store-bought decorations were a luxury most skipped. Families cut their own paper window flowers, brushed their own couplets, and pasted them up with rice paste. The work took an afternoon and gave the house its only fresh coat of the year.

Family Letters

For those working far from home, a letter was the closest thing to a seat at the table. Families read the old letters aloud and wrote new ones, so that even absent relatives had a place in the festival.

A Story from 1940

The meaning of the 1940 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Metal Dragon year.

Yan Ling, bicycle repairman, a coastal neighborhood in Guangzhou.

Meet Yan Ling, a bicycle repairman living in a coastal neighborhood in Guangzhou. 1940 was the year Yan Ling almost did not make it home. The train was late, the platform was crowded, and the journey that should have taken hours stretched into a full day. Yan Ling dozed standing up, woke to snack sellers pushing through the car, and watched the winter fields pass in the dark. When the train finally pulled in, the streets were already lit with red lanterns and the air smelled like firecrackers. Yan Ling reached the family door just as the reunion meal was being set out. There was no time to change, no time to rest. Yan Ling sat down still wearing the coat from the road, and the first bite of the meal was the best Yan Ling had tasted all year. The Metal Dragon year began with a simple reminder: the hardest part of the holiday is getting there, and the rest takes care of itself.

When was Chinese New Year in 1940?

How was the festival celebrated in 1940?

In 1940, families marked the festival with reunion dinners, ancestor offerings, firecrackers, red envelopes for children, spring couplets on doorways, and lion and dragon dances. The customs followed centuries-old tradition, with regional differences across China.
