Chinese New Year 1971 landed on January 27, kicking off the Year of the Metal Pig on the traditional lunisolar calendar. In the 60-year cycle of Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, this was the Xin (辛) year -- the Metal stem paired with the Pig branch.

The Pig holds a special place in the Chinese zodiac, and a Metal Pig year carries its own distinctive energy. Each zodiac year brings a different flavor to the Spring Festival, shaping the hopes and expectations people carry into the months ahead.

Spring Festival Customs in 1971

In 1971, as always, Spring Festival was shaped by the rituals people chose. From food to decoration to ceremony, each custom connected households to a shared heritage. Here are six of the most important customs observed during the Metal Pig year.

🎇

Firecrackers and Fireworks

Some families preferred long, winding strings of firecrackers hung from rooftops; others lit individual fireworks that sent colored sparks spiraling up into the dark sky. Both styles were equally beloved. In 1971, firecracker shops stayed open late, their shelves piled high with red paper tubes.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

Some households went further than cleaning. Walls were whitewashed, broken windowpanes were replaced, and the door itself got a fresh coat of red paint if one had been saved up. The goal was to make the house feel new. In 1971, these small home improvements were a kind of festival preparation in their own right.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

No preparation was too small. A new wooden chopping board, a fresh set of chopsticks, a jar of pickled vegetables from a trusted maker -- these small upgrades were part of welcoming the new year properly. In 1971, the shops that sold holiday specialties stayed open longer each evening.

👗

Wearing New Clothes

New clothing was more than vanity; it was symbolic. Starting the year in fresh garments represented starting the year with a clean appearance and a fresh spirit. For families of limited means, a single new item -- a scarf, a pair of socks, a small hat -- was enough to feel the tradition had been honored. In 1971, many children received just that.

🙏

Ancestor Worship

Ancestor worship was not a grand spectacle; it was the small things done consistently. A bowl of rice on the altar, a few sticks of incense left to burn down, paper spirit money folded carefully and set aside for burning. In 1971, these daily acts were what kept the connection to past generations alive.

🧵

Red Envelopes

Hongbao -- red envelopes containing money -- were given to children and unmarried young adults. The red color was believed to bring good luck and ward off evil spirits. In 1971, the amounts were modest, but the gesture carried deep meaning and warmth.

Core Meaning

The Metal Pig year carries a unique blend of symbolism -- renewal, vitality, and the promise of fresh starts. Chinese New Year in 1971, the Year of the Metal Pig, was celebrated with hopes that reflected the zodiac animal's traditional character and the element's particular energy. Each year in the Chinese lunisolar calendar has its own meaning. The Metal Pig year beginning in 1971 brought expectations shaped by centuries of tradition. For families celebrating in 1971, the Metal Pig year carried a specific set of wishes: for health, for prosperity, for safety, and for the quiet happiness of a home in order.

👪

Home and Hearth

At its heart, Chinese New Year is about coming together. The festival gives families a chance to honor their ancestors, strengthen bonds, and reset relationships before the new year begins. It is a deeply personal celebration wrapped in communal tradition.

🌿

Good Fortune

Prosperity is not only about money. In the Chinese tradition, a prosperous year is one where the family stays healthy, relationships stay warm, and there is enough food on the table. The Pig year in 1971 was celebrated with these hopes in mind.

⚙

The Year's Energy

Every combination of animal and element has its own character. Metal years reward discipline, sharp thinking, structured effort, and careful planning.

🌿

Prosperity and Abundance

In Chinese culture, the Pig represents abundant, generous prosperity -- wealth that is shared and enjoyed fully. Celebrating the Metal Pig's arrival was an invitation to welcome abundance into the home for the year ahead.

Historical Context of 1971

The world in 1971 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Metal Pig year began.

🌎

China in 1971

China in 1971 was in the midst of the Cultural Revolution. Traditional customs were actively suppressed, and many families celebrated Chinese New Year quietly or not at all. Red Guards discouraged temple fairs, lion dances, and ancestor worship. Yet the festival survived underground, kept alive by families who refused to let their traditions die.

🌐

The Cultural Revolution Era

The Cultural Revolution (1966-1976) was one of the most disruptive periods in Chinese history. Traditional culture was attacked, intellectuals were persecuted, and society was turned upside down. Chinese New Year was rebranded as a 'revolutionized' holiday, stripped of its religious and cultural elements. But beneath the surface, people still held onto the old ways.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

For families in 1971, celebrating Chinese New Year required discretion. A quiet meal at home, a whispered blessing for the ancestors, a red envelope passed under the table -- these small acts of cultural defiance kept the spirit of the festival alive during its darkest period.

📚

Tradition as Stability

The fact that Chinese New Year survived the Cultural Revolution is a testament to its deep roots in Chinese identity. No amount of political pressure could erase a tradition that had been practiced for thousands of years. When the Cultural Revolution ended, the festival emerged stronger than ever.

A Story from 1971

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Metal Pig year.

Meet Si Yuan, an artist in a rural community in Shandong. 1971 started quietly. The weeks before Chinese New Year were colder than usual, and Si spent most evenings at home helping a younger sibling practice writing auspicious characters on cheap red paper. The sibling was clumsy at first -- the ink ran, the characters were too large, the paper got wrinkled -- but by the third evening, the work started to look respectable. On New Year's Eve, when the time came to paste the couplets up, the family chose the sibling's best attempt rather than buying professionally written ones. The characters were a little wobbly, the ink was a little uneven, but the words were honest and meant something. Neighbors who walked past the door sometimes glanced twice at the writing, then smiled; they could tell it was a child's work, and they approved. Si would later say that this small act -- letting a child write something important -- was what the Metal Pig year meant for the family. The festival, after all, was not about perfection. It was about passing something on.
