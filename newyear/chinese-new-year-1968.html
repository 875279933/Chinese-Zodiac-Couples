Spring Festival Customs in 1968

The 1968 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Earth Monkey year.

  • 🎇

    Firecrackers

    At midnight the sky lit up. Long strings of firecrackers unrolled from rooftops, and children clapped hands over their ears. The noise chased the old year off. In 1968, the smoke hung low over the rooftops and mixed with the smell of cooking oil.

  • 🏯

    Temple Visits

    On the first morning, families walked to the temple with incense. The early arrivals hoped to be first to the altar. In 1968, temple courtyards filled with new clothes and low murmurs before sunrise.

  • 🔥

    Ancestor Offerings

    Before the meal, families set places for those who came before. Incense, a cup of wine, a bow. In 1968, the ancestor altar was the quiet center of the loudest week of the year.

  • 🧹

    Spring Cleaning

    A few days before the new year, every household swept, scrubbed, and mended. Windows came clean, old papers went out, and worn clothes got patched. The point was to step into the new year unburdened. In 1968, brooms went back in the corner before New Year's Day, since sweeping on the holiday itself was said to sweep the luck out.

  • 🎁

    Red Envelopes

    Married adults tucked crisp bills into red envelopes and pressed them into children's hands. The amount mattered less than the gesture. In 1968, a child might carry a stack of hongbao home and count them late into the night.

  • 👗

    New Clothes

    Red was the favorite, but any bright color would do. Children wore what made them happy; adults leaned toward darker, quieter tones. In 1968, a child in a new red coat walking to a relative's house was the picture of the holiday.

Paper Cuttings

Families kept the decorations going with scissors and red paper. Window flowers of the zodiac animal, the character for fortune turned upside down, and simple lucky phrases went up on every pane.

🏯

Quiet Temple Visits

Temple visits thinned out, but a few families still came at dawn to light a stick of incense. They stayed only a few minutes, yet those few minutes kept an old habit alive for the next generation.

🍲

Homemade Dumplings

The reunion meal leaned on what a household could make itself. Dumplings, rolled and folded around the table, became the center of the dinner, and the folding was the part children remembered.

✉

Letters Home

For those sent far away, a letter written before the eve was the closest thing to being there. Families read them aloud at the table and saved them in a box for the next year.

A Story from 1968

The meaning of the 1968 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Earth Monkey year.

Meet Lin Wei, a rail porter living in a market street in Xi'an. 1968 was supposed to be an ordinary Chinese New Year, but it became one Lin Wei would not forget. It started with a small disaster: the kitchen stove broke on New Year's Eve morning, right when the reunion meal was meant to be cooked. The house went quiet. Then neighbors stepped in. One lent a portable stove, another sent a pot of steaming rice, and a shopkeeper kept her door open an extra hour so the family could buy a replacement. By late afternoon the kitchen worked again, and by evening the table was full. The meal that year tasted better, maybe because it almost did not happen. Lin Wei took a simple lesson from the Earth Monkey year: good fortune often looks like neighbors looking out for each other. After midnight, with firecrackers rolling down the street, Lin Wei wrote a line in a notebook: remember the stove.

When was Chinese New Year in 1968?

How was the festival celebrated in 1968?

In 1968, families marked the festival with reunion dinners, ancestor offerings, firecrackers, red envelopes for children, spring couplets on doorways, and lion and dragon dances. The customs followed centuries-old tradition, with regional differences across China.
