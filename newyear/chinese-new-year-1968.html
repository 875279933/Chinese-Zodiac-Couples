Spring Festival Customs in 1968

How did people actually celebrate Chinese New Year in 1968? The customs were deeply rooted in centuries of tradition, with regional variations that made each celebration unique. This year, families focused on six favorite rituals -- each one chosen for its special meaning and its place in the household's routine.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

Before the new year arrived, every house was cleaned from top to bottom. The act of sweeping away dust was symbolic -- it removed the old year's bad luck and made space for fresh fortune. In 1968, even the busiest households found time for a thorough cleaning in the last few days of December.

🎇

Firecrackers and Fireworks

Firecrackers had a practical as well as a symbolic purpose. Their noise was meant to drive off anything unwelcome, leaving the house clean and auspicious for the coming year. In 1968, families set them off in doorways and courtyards, lighting one string after another until the ground glowed red with paper scraps.

👩

Visiting Relatives and Friends

Dressed in new clothes, families marched from one household to the next, bearing small gifts of fruit, candy, or homemade cakes. The host offered tea, passed around nuts, and exchanged a few words of blessing before sending everyone on their way. In 1968, a well-connected family could have three or four visits in a single day.

👗

Wearing New Clothes

New clothes for the new year were a firm tradition. A child who had worn the same jacket for months would be measured and taken to the tailor, or a bolt of cloth would be brought home so that a mother could sew something herself. In 1968, the first wearing of the new outfit happened on New Year's Day itself.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

In the week before the new year, markets overflowed. Housewives bought special ingredients, children were measured for new clothes, and every household stocked up on candies and nuts for visiting relatives. In 1968, the busiest market days felt almost crowded enough to be festive in themselves.

🏯

Temple Visits and Prayers

Making a wish at the temple was a serious but gentle act. Parents whispered quiet prayers for their children; grandparents prayed for the whole family's health. The phrases were simple and ancient. In 1968, the temple visit was still a regular part of the festival for millions of families.

Chinese New Year in 1968, the Year of the Earth Monkey, was celebrated with hopes that reflected the zodiac animal's traditional character and the element's particular energy. For families celebrating in 1968, the Earth Monkey year carried a specific set of wishes: for health, for prosperity, for safety, and for the quiet happiness of a home in order.

🔴

A Fresh Start

Each new lunar year offers a chance to begin again. In 1968, under the sign of the Monkey, families saw the festival as an invitation to reset their habits, their homes, and their hopes.

🏙

Wisdom of the Earth Element

The Earth element shapes the character of the year. Earth years are steady, practical, and good for saving, building, and long-term planning.

👪

Home and Hearth

At its heart, Chinese New Year is about coming together. The festival gives families a chance to honor their ancestors, strengthen bonds, and reset relationships before the new year begins. It is a deeply personal celebration wrapped in communal tradition.

🌿

Good Fortune

Prosperity is not only about money. In the Chinese tradition, a prosperous year is one where the family stays healthy, relationships stay warm, and there is enough food on the table. The Monkey year in 1968 was celebrated with these hopes in mind.

Historical Context of 1968

The world in 1968 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Earth Monkey year began.

🌎

China in 1968

China in 1968 was in the midst of the Cultural Revolution. Traditional customs were actively suppressed, and many families celebrated Chinese New Year quietly or not at all. Red Guards discouraged temple fairs, lion dances, and ancestor worship. Yet the festival survived underground, kept alive by families who refused to let their traditions die.

🌐

The Cultural Revolution Era

The Cultural Revolution (1966-1976) was one of the most disruptive periods in Chinese history. Traditional culture was attacked, intellectuals were persecuted, and society was turned upside down. Chinese New Year was rebranded as a 'revolutionized' holiday, stripped of its religious and cultural elements. But beneath the surface, people still held onto the old ways.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

For families in 1968, celebrating Chinese New Year required discretion. A quiet meal at home, a whispered blessing for the ancestors, a red envelope passed under the table -- these small acts of cultural defiance kept the spirit of the festival alive during its darkest period.

📚

Tradition as Stability

The fact that Chinese New Year survived the Cultural Revolution is a testament to its deep roots in Chinese identity. No amount of political pressure could erase a tradition that had been practiced for thousands of years. When the Cultural Revolution ended, the festival emerged stronger than ever.

A Story from 1968

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Earth Monkey year.

Meet Hui Lin, an assistant in an old quarter of Nanjing. 1968 started quietly. The weeks before Chinese New Year were colder than usual, and Hui spent most evenings at home helping a younger sibling practice writing auspicious characters on cheap red paper. The sibling was clumsy at first -- the ink ran, the characters were too large, the paper got wrinkled -- but by the third evening, the work started to look respectable. On New Year's Eve, when the time came to paste the couplets up, the family chose the sibling's best attempt rather than buying professionally written ones. The characters were a little wobbly, the ink was a little uneven, but the words were honest and meant something. Neighbors who walked past the door sometimes glanced twice at the writing, then smiled; they could tell it was a child's work, and they approved. Hui would later say that this small act -- letting a child write something important -- was what the Earth Monkey year meant for the family. The festival, after all, was not about perfection. It was about passing something on.
