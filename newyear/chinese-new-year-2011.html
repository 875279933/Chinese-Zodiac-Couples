Spring Festival Customs in 2011

The 2011 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Metal Rabbit year.

01

Visiting Relatives

The first days of the year were for rounds of visits. Married couples called on the wife's family on the second day. In 2011, the route was planned days ahead, and every stop came with tea, snacks, and good wishes.

02

Lantern Night

On the fifteenth night, lanterns went up. Children carried rabbits and fish made of paper and wire, candlelit from inside. In 2011, the festival closed the way it opened, with light.

03

Spring Couplets

Red paper strips went up on every door, black ink brushed in pairs, with a shorter line across the top. Door gods stood guard at the entrance. In 2011, a good calligrapher could fill half a street with orders in the days before the festival.

04

Lion and Dragon Dances

A lion dance troupe needed two strong dancers and a drummer. A dragon dance needed dozens, each holding a pole that held up the long body. In 2011, such performances drew the biggest crowds of the fair.

05

Ancestor Offerings

Before the meal, families set places for those who came before. Incense, a cup of wine, a bow. In 2011, the ancestor altar was the quiet center of the loudest week of the year.

06

Spring Cleaning

A few days before the new year, every household swept, scrubbed, and mended. Windows came clean, old papers went out, and worn clothes got patched. The point was to step into the new year unburdened. In 2011, brooms went back in the corner before New Year's Day, since sweeping on the holiday itself was said to sweep the luck out.

Abundance in Chinese tradition shows up through symbols. A fish on the table stands for having more than enough. Dumplings stand for wealth. Tangerines stand for good fortune. In 2011, these small signs were everywhere.

Home and Heritage

Cultural Context of 2011

The 2011 festival opened the Metal Rabbit year against the backdrop of its time. Here is a look at the cultural and social life families carried into the festival.

Cultural Snapshot: 2006-2015

Text Message Greetings

On New Year's Day, phones lit up with text greetings, sometimes arriving in batches. A household in 2011 might send the same message to a hundred contacts before lunch.

Online Shopping

Gift boxes, decorations, and even fresh ingredients came from online shops, delivered to the door in the days before the festival. The market trip shrank to a single afternoon.

Digital Red Envelopes

The first digital red envelopes appeared, sent phone to phone with a few taps. They did not replace the paper ones, but they reached relatives who lived too far to hand a bill to.

The Gala in HD

The television gala went high-definition, and the cameras grew more elaborate each year. Families still talked through most of it, but the midnight countdown stayed the one moment everyone watched.

A Story from 2011

The meaning of the 2011 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Metal Rabbit year.

Meet An Wei, a fish seller living in a riverside town in Suzhou. 2011 was the year An Wei finally understood what Chinese New Year was for. Until then, An Wei had cared mostly about new clothes, red envelopes, and a few days off. But that winter, the elder relative who usually hosted the reunion dinner fell ill. Without a host, the family briefly wondered whether the dinner would happen at all. Then, quietly, people stepped up. An aunt offered her kitchen. An uncle took over the shopping. A cousin, normally shy, found an old calligraphy set and wrote the spring couplets. By New Year's Eve the family was gathered around a different table in a different room, but everything important was the same. The dishes carried their old meanings, the altar was set with care, and the room stayed warm. For An Wei, the Metal Rabbit year was a lesson: tradition lives in people, not in places.

2011 Festival - Quick Answers

The basics on the 2011 festival, in short.

When was Chinese New Year in 2011?

The festival fell on February 3, 2011. It marked the start of the Year of the Metal Rabbit on the traditional Chinese lunisolar calendar.

What animal is 2011 in the Chinese zodiac?

2011 is the Year of the Rabbit, specifically the Metal Rabbit. The Metal Rabbit year ran from February 3, 2011 to January 22, 2012 on the Gregorian calendar.

How was the festival celebrated in 2011?

In 2011, families marked the festival with reunion dinners, ancestor offerings, firecrackers, red envelopes for children, spring couplets on doorways, and lion and dragon dances. The customs followed centuries-old tradition, with regional differences across China.

What element is 2011?

2011 is a Metal year. In the five-element cycle, Metal years bring their own character to the Rabbit sign, shaping the tone of the year that began on February 3, 2011.
