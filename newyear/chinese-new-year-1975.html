Festival Positioning

Chinese New Year 1975 marked the transition into the Year of the Wood Rabbit, a year shaped by the energy of both the Wood element and the Rabbit's distinctive character.

Chinese New Year 1975 landed on February 11, kicking off the Year of the Wood Rabbit on the traditional lunisolar calendar. In the 60-year cycle of Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, this was the Yi (乙) year -- the Wood stem paired with the Rabbit branch.

The Rabbit holds a special place in the Chinese zodiac, and a Wood Rabbit year carries its own distinctive energy. Each zodiac year brings a different flavor to the Spring Festival, shaping the hopes and expectations people carry into the months ahead.

Spring Festival Customs in 1975

In 1975, as always, Spring Festival was shaped by the rituals people chose. From food to decoration to ceremony, each custom connected households to a shared heritage. Here are six of the most important customs observed during the Wood Rabbit year.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

In the week before the new year, markets overflowed. Housewives bought special ingredients, children were measured for new clothes, and every household stocked up on candies and nuts for visiting relatives. In 1975, the busiest market days felt almost crowded enough to be festive in themselves.

🎓

Reunion Dinner

The New Year's Eve dinner was the centerpiece of the celebration. Families gathered around tables laden with dishes chosen for their symbolic meaning -- fish for surplus, dumplings for wealth, and sticky rice cake for a higher year ahead. In 1975, this meal was often the most elaborate of the entire year.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

Spring cleaning was not just about dirt. Windows were washed, old newspapers were cleared, worn-out clothes were mended or replaced, and kitchens were scrubbed. The idea was to start the new year with an uncluttered home. In 1975, the cleaning was done a day or two before the festival so that the brooms could be put away before New Year's Day -- sweeping on the holiday itself was considered bad luck.

🎇

Firecrackers and Fireworks

Setting off firecrackers at midnight was (and still is) one of the most thrilling parts of Chinese New Year. The loud bangs were meant to scare away the mythical beast Nian and any lingering bad luck from the old year. In smaller towns and villages in 1975, the sound echoed through the night sky for hours.

🍭

New Year Sweets and Snacks

Tea was the centerpiece of every holiday visit. A well-made pot of tea, poured into small cups and offered with both hands, was a sign of welcome and respect. In 1975, households bought a little more tea than usual, specifically for the stream of visitors expected in the first week of the new year.

👗

Wearing New Clothes

New clothing was more than vanity; it was symbolic. Starting the year in fresh garments represented starting the year with a clean appearance and a fresh spirit. For families of limited means, a single new item -- a scarf, a pair of socks, a small hat -- was enough to feel the tradition had been honored. In 1975, many children received just that.

Core Meaning

The Wood Rabbit year carries a unique blend of symbolism -- renewal, vitality, and the promise of fresh starts. Chinese New Year in 1975, the Year of the Wood Rabbit, was celebrated with hopes that reflected the zodiac animal's traditional character and the element's particular energy. Each year in the Chinese lunisolar calendar has its own meaning. The Wood Rabbit year beginning in 1975 brought expectations shaped by centuries of tradition. For families celebrating in 1975, the Wood Rabbit year carried a specific set of wishes: for health, for prosperity, for safety, and for the quiet happiness of a home in order.

🌳

Wood Element Energy

Wood brings the qualities of its element to the Rabbit year. Wood years favor growth, expansion, new connections, and creative development.

🌳

Wisdom of the Wood Element

The Wood element shapes the character of the year. Wood years favor growth, expansion, new connections, and creative development.

🌿

Good Fortune

Prosperity is not only about money. In the Chinese tradition, a prosperous year is one where the family stays healthy, relationships stay warm, and there is enough food on the table. The Rabbit year in 1975 was celebrated with these hopes in mind.

👪

Family and Heritage

The festival renews family ties. Children see their grandparents, siblings travel long distances to sit at the same table, and relatives who have been out of touch exchange warm greetings. In 1975, this renewal was the deepest purpose of the celebration.

Historical Context of 1975

The world in 1975 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Wood Rabbit year began.

🌎

China in 1975

China in 1975 was in the midst of the Cultural Revolution. Traditional customs were actively suppressed, and many families celebrated Chinese New Year quietly or not at all. Red Guards discouraged temple fairs, lion dances, and ancestor worship. Yet the festival survived underground, kept alive by families who refused to let their traditions die.

🌐

The Cultural Revolution Era

The Cultural Revolution (1966-1976) was one of the most disruptive periods in Chinese history. Traditional culture was attacked, intellectuals were persecuted, and society was turned upside down. Chinese New Year was rebranded as a 'revolutionized' holiday, stripped of its religious and cultural elements. But beneath the surface, people still held onto the old ways.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

For families in 1975, celebrating Chinese New Year required discretion. A quiet meal at home, a whispered blessing for the ancestors, a red envelope passed under the table -- these small acts of cultural defiance kept the spirit of the festival alive during its darkest period.

📚

Tradition as Stability

The fact that Chinese New Year survived the Cultural Revolution is a testament to its deep roots in Chinese identity. No amount of political pressure could erase a tradition that had been practiced for thousands of years. When the Cultural Revolution ended, the festival emerged stronger than ever.

A Story from 1975

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Wood Rabbit year.

Meet De Ming, an artist living in a rice-growing village in Hunan. 1975 was the year De finally understood what Chinese New Year was really about. Until then, he had mostly cared about new clothes, red envelopes, and a few days away from work. But 1975 was different. That winter, an elderly relative who usually hosted the festival dinner became ill. Without a clear host, the family briefly wondered whether the celebration would happen at all. Then, quietly, people stepped in. An aunt volunteered her kitchen. An uncle organized the grocery shopping. A cousin, normally shy, offered to write the spring couplets after finding an old calligraphy set. By New Year's Eve, the family was gathered around a different table in a different room, but everything important was the same. The dishes were symbolic, the atmosphere was warm, and the ancestor altar was prepared with as much care as ever. For De, it was a lesson in what the Wood Rabbit year really offered: not a guarantee of comfort, but a reminder that family could adapt and that tradition was ultimately carried in people, not places.
