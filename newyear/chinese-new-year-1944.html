Spring Festival Customs in 1944

The 1944 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Wood Monkey year.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

A few days before the new year, every household swept, scrubbed, and mended. Windows came clean, old papers went out, and worn clothes got patched. The point was to step into the new year unburdened. In 1944, brooms went back in the corner before New Year's Day, since sweeping on the holiday itself was said to sweep the luck out.

🎇

Firecrackers

At midnight the sky lit up. Long strings of firecrackers unrolled from rooftops, and children clapped hands over their ears. The noise chased the old year off. In 1944, the smoke hung low over the rooftops and mixed with the smell of cooking oil.

🏯

Temple Visits

On the first morning, families walked to the temple with incense. The early arrivals hoped to be first to the altar. In 1944, temple courtyards filled with new clothes and low murmurs before sunrise.

🔥

Ancestor Offerings

Before the meal, families set places for those who came before. Incense, a cup of wine, a bow. In 1944, the ancestor altar was the quiet center of the loudest week of the year.

🍲

Reunion Dinner

The night before the new year, families gathered around one table. Fish for surplus, dumplings for wealth, long noodles for long life, a chicken left whole to keep the family whole. In 1944, this was the meal people traveled all year to reach.

🎁

Red Envelopes

Married adults tucked crisp bills into red envelopes and pressed them into children's hands. The amount mattered less than the gesture. In 1944, a child might carry a stack of hongbao home and count them late into the night.

Core Meaning

The 1944 festival brought the Wood Monkey year, with its own blend of meaning. Each year in the Chinese lunisolar calendar carries its own character, and the Wood Monkey year that began on January 26, 1944 set the tone for the months ahead. For families, it carried a set of steady wishes: health, a full table, safety, and a home in good order. Read more on the Year of the Monkey.

The Monkey Character

The Monkey is clever and restless. Quick-witted, playful, and good with a workaround. A Monkey year rewards flexibility and a sense of humor.

🌿

Symbols on the Table

Abundance in Chinese tradition shows up through symbols. A fish on the table stands for having more than enough. Dumplings stand for wealth. Tangerines stand for good fortune. In 1944, these small signs were everywhere.

🌲

The Wood Element

Wood years grow. They favor patience, learning, and steady expansion, the way a tree adds rings each season.

👪

Home and Heritage

At its center, Chinese New Year is about coming together. The festival gives families a chance to honor the people who came before, mend the ties that frayed, and reset the household before the year turns.

Cultural Context of 1944

The 1944 festival opened the Wood Monkey year against the backdrop of its time. Here is a look at the cultural and social life families carried into the festival.

Cultural Snapshot: 1936-1945
🎈

Temple Fairs

The temple fair was the social event of the season. Stalls sold sugar figures, dried fruit, and paper toys, while opera stages drew crowds from early morning. In 1944, the fair was where a child spent their first red-envelope money.

🛍

Market Life

In the week before the new year, the market ran late into the evening. Shoppers bargained over cloth, fish, and fireworks while lanterns swung overhead. The lanes stayed loud until the last family had its supplies home.

✂

Homemade Decorations

Store-bought decorations were a luxury most skipped. Families cut their own paper window flowers, brushed their own couplets, and pasted them up with rice paste. The work took an afternoon and gave the house its only fresh coat of the year.

✉

Family Letters

For those working far from home, a letter was the closest thing to a seat at the table. Families read the old letters aloud and wrote new ones, so that even absent relatives had a place in the festival.

A Story from 1944

The meaning of the 1944 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Wood Monkey year.

Meet Lin Wei, a rail porter living in a market street in Xi'an. 1944 was supposed to be an ordinary Chinese New Year, but it became one Lin Wei would not forget. It started with a small disaster: the kitchen stove broke on New Year's Eve morning, right when the reunion meal was meant to be cooked. The house went quiet. Then neighbors stepped in. One lent a portable stove, another sent a pot of steaming rice, and a shopkeeper kept her door open an extra hour so the family could buy a replacement. By late afternoon the kitchen worked again, and by evening the table was full. The meal that year tasted better, maybe because it almost did not happen. Lin Wei took a simple lesson from the Wood Monkey year: good fortune often looks like neighbors looking out for each other. After midnight, with firecrackers rolling down the street, Lin Wei wrote a line in a notebook: remember the stove.
