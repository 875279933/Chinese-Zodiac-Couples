Spring Festival Customs in 2023

The 2023 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Water Rabbit year.

01

Ancestor Offerings

Before the meal, families set places for those who came before. Incense, a cup of wine, a bow. In 2023, the ancestor altar was the quiet center of the loudest week of the year.

02

Spring Cleaning

A few days before the new year, every household swept, scrubbed, and mended. Windows came clean, old papers went out, and worn clothes got patched. The point was to step into the new year unburdened. In 2023, brooms went back in the corner before New Year's Day, since sweeping on the holiday itself was said to sweep the luck out.

03

Firecrackers

At midnight the sky lit up. Long strings of firecrackers unrolled from rooftops, and children clapped hands over their ears. The noise chased the old year off. In 2023, the smoke hung low over the rooftops and mixed with the smell of cooking oil.

04

Temple Visits

On the first morning, families walked to the temple with incense. The early arrivals hoped to be first to the altar. In 2023, temple courtyards filled with new clothes and low murmurs before sunrise.

05

New Year Shopping

In the days before the festival, the market ran late. Stalls of red paper, dried fruit, fireworks, and cloth crowded the lanes. In 2023, shoppers carried bundles home through streets strung with lanterns.

06

Reunion Dinner

The night before the new year, families gathered around one table. Fish for surplus, dumplings for wealth, long noodles for long life, a chicken left whole to keep the family whole. In 2023, this was the meal people traveled all year to reach.

A Story from 2023

The meaning of the 2023 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Water Rabbit year.

A postcard from 2023

Meet An Wei, a fish seller living in a riverside town in Suzhou. 2023 was the year An Wei finally understood what Chinese New Year was for. Until then, An Wei had cared mostly about new clothes, red envelopes, and a few days off. But that winter, the elder relative who usually hosted the reunion dinner fell ill. Without a host, the family briefly wondered whether the dinner would happen at all. Then, quietly, people stepped up. An aunt offered her kitchen. An uncle took over the shopping. A cousin, normally shy, found an old calligraphy set and wrote the spring couplets. By New Year's Eve the family was gathered around a different table in a different room, but everything important was the same. The dishes carried their old meanings, the altar was set with care, and the room stayed warm. For An Wei, the Water Rabbit year was a lesson: tradition lives in people, not in places.
