Festival Positioning

Chinese New Year 1945 marked the transition into the Year of the Wood Rooster, a year shaped by the energy of both the Wood element and the Rooster's distinctive character.

Chinese New Year 1945 landed on February 13, kicking off the Year of the Wood Rooster on the traditional lunisolar calendar. In the 60-year cycle of Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, this was the Yi (乙) year -- the Wood stem paired with the Rooster branch.

The Rooster holds a special place in the Chinese zodiac, and a Wood Rooster year carries its own distinctive energy. Each zodiac year brings a different flavor to the Spring Festival, shaping the hopes and expectations people carry into the months ahead.

Spring Festival Customs in 1945

How did people actually celebrate Chinese New Year in 1945? The customs were deeply rooted in centuries of tradition, with regional variations that made each celebration unique. This year, families focused on six favorite rituals -- each one chosen for its special meaning and its place in the household's routine.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

Some households went further than cleaning. Walls were whitewashed, broken windowpanes were replaced, and the door itself got a fresh coat of red paint if one had been saved up. The goal was to make the house feel new. In 1945, these small home improvements were a kind of festival preparation in their own right.

🙏

Ancestor Worship

Before the dinner, the family gathered quietly before the ancestor altar. Bowing three times, lighting incense, placing small cups of tea and rice wine -- these were gestures of remembrance. In 1945, even families that rarely discussed the past still found time for this quiet ceremony.

👗

Wearing New Clothes

Some families set aside a special item each year -- a silk scarf for the daughter, a woolen vest for the son, a sturdy pair of shoes for the father. These items were worn on festival days and stored carefully for the rest of the year. In 1945, such pieces were sometimes the only new clothing a family bought.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

In the week before the new year, markets overflowed. Housewives bought special ingredients, children were measured for new clothes, and every household stocked up on candies and nuts for visiting relatives. In 1945, the busiest market days felt almost crowded enough to be festive in themselves.

🍭

New Year Sweets and Snacks

The 'eight treasures' box was a holiday favorite. Eight small compartments held eight different treats, each chosen for its auspicious name or shape. Guests were invited to help themselves, and children were sometimes allowed a second helping if they had been well behaved. In 1945, such boxes were often hand-painted and passed down through families.

🎓

Reunion Dinner

Nothing mattered more on New Year's Eve than sitting down together as a family. The table filled up slowly -- a whole steamed fish at one end, dumplings folded that morning at the other, a golden nian gao waiting for dessert. In 1945, mothers spent all day in the kitchen, and children were told to eat every last bite.

Core Meaning

The Wood Rooster year carries a unique blend of symbolism -- renewal, vitality, and the promise of fresh starts. Chinese New Year in 1945, the Year of the Wood Rooster, was celebrated with hopes that reflected the zodiac animal's traditional character and the element's particular energy. For families celebrating in 1945, the Wood Rooster year carried a specific set of wishes: for health, for prosperity, for safety, and for the quiet happiness of a home in order.

🌳

Wood Element Energy

Wood brings the qualities of its element to the Rooster year. Wood years favor growth, expansion, new connections, and creative development.

👪

Family and Heritage

The festival renews family ties. Children see their grandparents, siblings travel long distances to sit at the same table, and relatives who have been out of touch exchange warm greetings. In 1945, this renewal was the deepest purpose of the celebration.

🌿

The Rooster's Character

The Rooster has long been associated with hard work, punctuality, and a conscientious approach to life's duties. Celebrating the festival in this year was a way of recognizing those qualities and hoping to see more of them in the months ahead.

Historical Context of 1945

The world in 1945 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Wood Rooster year began.

🌎

China in 1945

China in 1945 was in the midst of profound upheaval. The Second Sino-Japanese War and World War II dominated daily life for millions. Despite the devastation, families still found ways to observe Chinese New Year, often in shelters or displaced communities. The festival became a symbol of endurance -- a reminder that life and tradition would outlast the conflict.

🌐

A World at War

Globally, 1945 fell within the era of World War II. Nations were consumed by conflict, economies were mobilized for war, and ordinary people faced rationing, displacement, and loss. For Chinese communities abroad, celebrating the new year carried an added layer of longing for homeland and hope for peace.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

For ordinary Chinese families in 1945, the Spring Festival was one of the few moments when the hardships of war could be set aside, even briefly. People made do with what they had -- a simple meal, a few firecrackers, a handwritten couplet. The determination to celebrate, no matter how modest, spoke to the deep resilience of Chinese culture.

📚

Tradition as Stability

In times of extreme uncertainty, the rituals of Chinese New Year provided a rare sense of normalcy. The ancestor worship, the reunion dinners, the red decorations -- these practices connected people to their heritage and to each other. They were a lifeline to identity when everything else felt fragile.

A Story from 1945

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Wood Rooster year.

Meet Dan Dan, a doctor born in a village outside Guangzhou. 1945 was supposed to be an ordinary Chinese New Year, but it turned into one of the holidays Dan would remember forever. It started with a small disaster: the stove in the family kitchen broke on New Year's Eve morning, right before the holiday meal was supposed to be cooked. The house felt suddenly, unexpectedly quiet. But neighbors rallied. One lent a portable stove; another sent over a large pot of already-steaming rice. A nearby shopkeeper kept his doors open a little longer just so the family could buy a replacement. By mid-afternoon, the kitchen was working again, if a little awkwardly, and by evening the dining table was full of dishes after all. The meal that year tasted better than usual, perhaps because it had nearly not happened. Dan learned something simple about the Wood Rooster year: that good fortune sometimes looks like ordinary people helping each other out. After midnight, as the firecrackers went off up and down the street, Dan wrote a short note in a notebook -- 'Remember the stove.'
