Spring Festival Customs in 2025

The 2025 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Wood Snake year.

01

Lantern Night

On the fifteenth night, lanterns went up. Children carried rabbits and fish made of paper and wire, candlelit from inside. In 2025, the festival closed the way it opened, with light.

02

Spring Couplets

Red paper strips went up on every door, black ink brushed in pairs, with a shorter line across the top. Door gods stood guard at the entrance. In 2025, a good calligrapher could fill half a street with orders in the days before the festival.

03

Lion and Dragon Dances

A lion dance troupe needed two strong dancers and a drummer. A dragon dance needed dozens, each holding a pole that held up the long body. In 2025, such performances drew the biggest crowds of the fair.

04

Visiting Relatives

The first days of the year were for rounds of visits. Married couples called on the wife's family on the second day. In 2025, the route was planned days ahead, and every stop came with tea, snacks, and good wishes.

05

Spring Cleaning

A few days before the new year, every household swept, scrubbed, and mended. Windows came clean, old papers went out, and worn clothes got patched. The point was to step into the new year unburdened. In 2025, brooms went back in the corner before New Year's Day, since sweeping on the holiday itself was said to sweep the luck out.

06

Firecrackers

At midnight the sky lit up. Long strings of firecrackers unrolled from rooftops, and children clapped hands over their ears. The noise chased the old year off. In 2025, the smoke hung low over the rooftops and mixed with the smell of cooking oil.

A Story from 2025

The meaning of the 2025 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Wood Snake year.

A postcard from 2025

Meet Dong Hai, a woodcutter living in a cold quarter in Harbin. 2025 was supposed to be an ordinary Chinese New Year, but it became one Dong Hai would not forget. It started with a small disaster: the kitchen stove broke on New Year's Eve morning, right when the reunion meal was meant to be cooked. The house went quiet. Then neighbors stepped in. One lent a portable stove, another sent a pot of steaming rice, and a shopkeeper kept her door open an extra hour so the family could buy a replacement. By late afternoon the kitchen worked again, and by evening the table was full. The meal that year tasted better, maybe because it almost did not happen. Dong Hai took a simple lesson from the Wood Snake year: good fortune often looks like neighbors looking out for each other. After midnight, with firecrackers rolling down the street, Dong Hai wrote a line in a notebook: remember the stove.
