Spring Festival Customs in 1965

The 1965 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Wood Snake year.

01

Lion and Dragon Dances

A lion dance troupe needed two strong dancers and a drummer. A dragon dance needed dozens, each holding a pole that held up the long body. In 1965, such performances drew the biggest crowds of the fair.

02

Visiting Relatives

The first days of the year were for rounds of visits. Married couples called on the wife's family on the second day. In 1965, the route was planned days ahead, and every stop came with tea, snacks, and good wishes.

03

Lantern Night

On the fifteenth night, lanterns went up. Children carried rabbits and fish made of paper and wire, candlelit from inside. In 1965, the festival closed the way it opened, with light.

04

Spring Couplets

Red paper strips went up on every door, black ink brushed in pairs, with a shorter line across the top. Door gods stood guard at the entrance. In 1965, a good calligrapher could fill half a street with orders in the days before the festival.

05

Temple Visits

On the first morning, families walked to the temple with incense. The early arrivals hoped to be first to the altar. In 1965, temple courtyards filled with new clothes and low murmurs before sunrise.

06

Ancestor Offerings

Before the meal, families set places for those who came before. Incense, a cup of wine, a bow. In 1965, the ancestor altar was the quiet center of the loudest week of the year.

🐍

The Snake Character

The Snake values thought over speed. Observant, private, and rarely wasteful. A Snake year favors households that plan quietly and act deliberately.

🌿

Symbols on the Table

Abundance in Chinese tradition shows up through symbols. A fish on the table stands for having more than enough. Dumplings stand for wealth. Tangerines stand for good fortune. In 1965, these small signs were everywhere.

🌲

The Wood Element

Wood years grow. They favor patience, learning, and steady expansion, the way a tree adds rings each season.

👪

Home and Heritage

At its center, Chinese New Year is about coming together. The festival gives families a chance to honor the people who came before, mend the ties that frayed, and reset the household before the year turns.

Cultural Context of 1965

The 1965 festival opened the Wood Snake year against the backdrop of its time. Here is a look at the cultural and social life families carried into the festival.

Cultural Snapshot: 1956-1965

Shared Stoves

In many neighborhoods, families cooked on shared stoves set up in the lane. The reunion meal came together dish by dish as each household contributed something, and the smell of braised meat drifted through the whole street.

Shift Holidays

Factories and work units arranged time off in shifts so that some families ate on the eve and others on the first day. The exact hour mattered less than the fact that the meal happened at all.

Handmade Lanterns

Store-bought lanterns were hard to find, so fathers and grandfathers built them from bamboo and paper. A child in 1965 carried a lantern shaped like a rabbit that had taken three evenings to make.

Radio Evenings

With a radio in the room, the family could hear opera and storytelling while they folded dumplings. The set was small and the signal came and went, but it gave the evening a soundtrack.

A Story from 1965

The meaning of the 1965 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Wood Snake year.

Meet Dong Hai, a woodcutter living in a cold quarter in Harbin. 1965 was supposed to be an ordinary Chinese New Year, but it became one Dong Hai would not forget. It started with a small disaster: the kitchen stove broke on New Year's Eve morning, right when the reunion meal was meant to be cooked. The house went quiet. Then neighbors stepped in. One lent a portable stove, another sent a pot of steaming rice, and a shopkeeper kept her door open an extra hour so the family could buy a replacement. By late afternoon the kitchen worked again, and by evening the table was full. The meal that year tasted better, maybe because it almost did not happen. Dong Hai took a simple lesson from the Wood Snake year: good fortune often looks like neighbors looking out for each other. After midnight, with firecrackers rolling down the street, Dong Hai wrote a line in a notebook: remember the stove.
