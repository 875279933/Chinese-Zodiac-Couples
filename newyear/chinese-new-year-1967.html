Spring Festival Customs in 1967

How did people actually celebrate Chinese New Year in 1967? The customs were deeply rooted in centuries of tradition, with regional variations that made each celebration unique. This year, families focused on six favorite rituals -- each one chosen for its special meaning and its place in the household's routine.

🧵

Red Envelopes

A red envelope is more than money; it is a transfer of good fortune from one generation to the next. The giver passes on a wish for health, safety, and prosperity. In 1967, this tradition was observed quietly at home after the reunion meal, one child at a time.

🍭

New Year Sweets and Snacks

Making the holiday snacks was a task shared by the women of the household. Rolling sesame cookies, pressing rice cakes, stirring the sugar syrup that coated candied fruit -- these were the smells and sounds of preparation. In 1967, a jar of the family's favorite sweets sat on every kitchen table.

🎁

Spring Couplets and Door Gods

Families pasted red paper couplets with auspicious phrases on their doorways, along with images of door gods to protect the household. Writing or buying new couplets before the new year was an essential preparation, and the best calligraphers in the village were always in high demand in 1967.

👗

Wearing New Clothes

Red was the preferred color for festival wear, but any cheerful color was welcome. Adults tended toward darker, more dignified outfits; children wore whatever made them happy. In 1967, a child in a new red coat walking to a relative's house was the characteristic image of the holiday.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

Preparation started early. Houses were cleaned from top to bottom, debts were settled where possible, and new items were bought so that nothing worn or broken carried into the new year. Shopping in 1967 was a long, slow pleasure -- a chance to stroll, to bargain gently, and to plan every detail of the coming festival.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

Some households went further than cleaning. Walls were whitewashed, broken windowpanes were replaced, and the door itself got a fresh coat of red paint if one had been saved up. The goal was to make the house feel new. In 1967, these small home improvements were a kind of festival preparation in their own right.

Historical Context of 1967

The world in 1967 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Fire Goat year began.

🌎

China in 1967

China in 1967 was in the midst of the Cultural Revolution. Traditional customs were actively suppressed, and many families celebrated Chinese New Year quietly or not at all. Red Guards discouraged temple fairs, lion dances, and ancestor worship. Yet the festival survived underground, kept alive by families who refused to let their traditions die.

🌐

The Cultural Revolution Era

The Cultural Revolution (1966-1976) was one of the most disruptive periods in Chinese history. Traditional culture was attacked, intellectuals were persecuted, and society was turned upside down. Chinese New Year was rebranded as a 'revolutionized' holiday, stripped of its religious and cultural elements. But beneath the surface, people still held onto the old ways.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

For families in 1967, celebrating Chinese New Year required discretion. A quiet meal at home, a whispered blessing for the ancestors, a red envelope passed under the table -- these small acts of cultural defiance kept the spirit of the festival alive during its darkest period.

📚

Tradition as Stability

The fact that Chinese New Year survived the Cultural Revolution is a testament to its deep roots in Chinese identity. No amount of political pressure could erase a tradition that had been practiced for thousands of years. When the Cultural Revolution ended, the festival emerged stronger than ever.

A Story from 1967

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Fire Goat year.

Meet Gao Ming, an artist born in a village outside Guangzhou. 1967 was supposed to be an ordinary Chinese New Year, but it turned into one of the holidays Gao would remember forever. It started with a small disaster: the stove in the family kitchen broke on New Year's Eve morning, right before the holiday meal was supposed to be cooked. The house felt suddenly, unexpectedly quiet. But neighbors rallied. One lent a portable stove; another sent over a large pot of already-steaming rice. A nearby shopkeeper kept his doors open a little longer just so the family could buy a replacement. By mid-afternoon, the kitchen was working again, if a little awkwardly, and by evening the dining table was full of dishes after all. The meal that year tasted better than usual, perhaps because it had nearly not happened. Gao learned something simple about the Fire Goat year: that good fortune sometimes looks like ordinary people helping each other out. After midnight, as the firecrackers went off up and down the street, Gao wrote a short note in a notebook -- 'Remember the stove.'
