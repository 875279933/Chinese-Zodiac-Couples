Chinese New Year 1978 landed on February 7, kicking off the Year of the Earth Horse on the traditional lunisolar calendar. In the 60-year cycle of Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, this was the Wu (戊) year -- the Earth stem paired with the Horse branch.

The Horse holds a special place in the Chinese zodiac, and a Earth Horse year carries its own distinctive energy. Each zodiac year brings a different flavor to the Spring Festival, shaping the hopes and expectations people carry into the months ahead.

Spring Festival Customs in 1978

Chinese New Year customs in 1978 mixed timeless traditions with small, personal touches. Different households emphasized different things, but six practices stood out as central to how the festival was lived that winter. Here is what a typical family's season might have included.

🙏

Ancestor Worship

Some families visited graves before the holiday, cleaning the stones and leaving offerings. Others held the ceremony at home, around a table with photographs and incense. Either way, the intention was the same: to say thank you. In 1978, the quiet act of remembrance felt more important than any public celebration.

🏯

Temple Visits and Prayers

Temple visits were not only about asking for things. They were also about thankfulness -- for a safe year past, for the health of family members, for enough to eat. Families lit three sticks of incense each, bowed before the main altar, and sometimes left small offerings of fruit or oil. In 1978, the larger temples held special ceremonies throughout the first week.

🎁

Spring Couplets and Door Gods

Upstairs windows got smaller red decorations, too -- the inverted character for fortune, paper cuttings of the zodiac animal, and simple lucky phrases. The effect was a house that glowed red from the outside. In 1978, preparing all this paper took a full afternoon.

🧵

Red Envelopes

Hongbao -- red envelopes containing money -- were given to children and unmarried young adults. The red color was believed to bring good luck and ward off evil spirits. In 1978, the amounts were modest, but the gesture carried deep meaning and warmth.

🎇

Firecrackers and Fireworks

As midnight approached, the crackle of firecrackers rolled through every neighborhood. Children covered their ears and laughed; elders lit the longest strings they could afford. The smoke hung low over the rooftops, mixing with the smell of cooking oil and incense. In 1978, a village without fireworks on New Year's Eve would have felt unfinished.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

No preparation was too small. A new wooden chopping board, a fresh set of chopsticks, a jar of pickled vegetables from a trusted maker -- these small upgrades were part of welcoming the new year properly. In 1978, the shops that sold holiday specialties stayed open longer each evening.

Core Meaning

The Earth Horse year carries a unique blend of symbolism -- renewal, vitality, and the promise of fresh starts. Chinese New Year in 1978, the Year of the Earth Horse, was celebrated with hopes that reflected the zodiac animal's traditional character and the element's particular energy. Each year in the Chinese lunisolar calendar has its own meaning. The Earth Horse year beginning in 1978 brought expectations shaped by centuries of tradition. For families celebrating in 1978, the Earth Horse year carried a specific set of wishes: for health, for prosperity, for safety, and for the quiet happiness of a home in order.

🌿

Prosperity and Abundance

In Chinese culture, the Horse represents dynamic prosperity -- opportunities that arrive quickly and require bold action. Celebrating the Earth Horse's arrival was an invitation to welcome abundance into the home for the year ahead.

👪

Home and Hearth

At its heart, Chinese New Year is about coming together. The festival gives families a chance to honor their ancestors, strengthen bonds, and reset relationships before the new year begins. It is a deeply personal celebration wrapped in communal tradition.

🔴

A Fresh Start

Each new lunar year offers a chance to begin again. In 1978, under the sign of the Horse, families saw the festival as an invitation to reset their habits, their homes, and their hopes.

🌿

Good Fortune

Prosperity is not only about money. In the Chinese tradition, a prosperous year is one where the family stays healthy, relationships stay warm, and there is enough food on the table. The Horse year in 1978 was celebrated with these hopes in mind.

Historical Context of 1978

The world in 1978 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Earth Horse year began.

🌎

China in 1978

China in 1978 was emerging from the Cultural Revolution and entering a new era under Deng Xiaoping's reform policies. The country was opening up, and traditional customs like Chinese New Year were being revived after years of suppression. For the first time in a decade, people could celebrate openly and with genuine enthusiasm.

🌐

Reform and Opening Up

The late 1970s marked a turning point for China. Deng Xiaoping's reforms began to transform the economy and society. Traditional festivals, including Chinese New Year, were no longer discouraged. Instead, they were embraced as part of China's cultural heritage. The Spring Festival of 1978 was a celebration not just of the new year, but of freedom.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

After years of restraint, families in 1978 celebrated Chinese New Year with renewed energy. Markets filled with holiday goods, temples reopened, and lion dances returned to the streets. The festival became a symbol of the new era -- vibrant, hopeful, and unapologetically Chinese.

📚

Tradition as Stability

The revival of Chinese New Year in the late 1970s showed how deeply embedded the festival is in Chinese life. It wasn't just a holiday -- it was an expression of identity. After the Cultural Revolution, reclaiming these traditions was a way for people to reconnect with their heritage.

A Story from 1978

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Earth Horse year.

Meet Ah Chen, an assistant living in a small town in southern China. 1978 was the year Ah finally understood what Chinese New Year was really about. Until then, she had mostly cared about new clothes, red envelopes, and a few days away from work. But 1978 was different. That winter, an elderly relative who usually hosted the festival dinner became ill. Without a clear host, the family briefly wondered whether the celebration would happen at all. Then, quietly, people stepped in. An aunt volunteered her kitchen. An uncle organized the grocery shopping. A cousin, normally shy, offered to write the spring couplets after finding an old calligraphy set. By New Year's Eve, the family was gathered around a different table in a different room, but everything important was the same. The dishes were symbolic, the atmosphere was warm, and the ancestor altar was prepared with as much care as ever. For Ah, it was a lesson in what the Earth Horse year really offered: not a guarantee of comfort, but a reminder that family could adapt and that tradition was ultimately carried in people, not places.
