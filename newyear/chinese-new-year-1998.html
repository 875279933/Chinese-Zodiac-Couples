Chinese New Year 1998 landed on January 28, kicking off the Year of the Earth Tiger on the traditional lunisolar calendar. In the 60-year cycle of Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches, this was the Wu (戊) year -- the Earth stem paired with the Tiger branch.

Spring Festival Customs in 1998

In 1998, as always, Spring Festival was shaped by the rituals people chose. From food to decoration to ceremony, each custom connected households to a shared heritage. Here are six of the most important customs observed during the Earth Tiger year.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

Spring cleaning was not just about dirt. Windows were washed, old newspapers were cleared, worn-out clothes were mended or replaced, and kitchens were scrubbed. The idea was to start the new year with an uncluttered home. In 1998, the cleaning was done a day or two before the festival so that the brooms could be put away before New Year's Day -- sweeping on the holiday itself was considered bad luck.

🎓

Reunion Dinner

Nothing mattered more on New Year's Eve than sitting down together as a family. The table filled up slowly -- a whole steamed fish at one end, dumplings folded that morning at the other, a golden nian gao waiting for dessert. In 1998, mothers spent all day in the kitchen, and children were told to eat every last bite.

🍭

New Year Sweets and Snacks

Tea was the centerpiece of every holiday visit. A well-made pot of tea, poured into small cups and offered with both hands, was a sign of welcome and respect. In 1998, households bought a little more tea than usual, specifically for the stream of visitors expected in the first week of the new year.

🏯

Temple Visits and Prayers

On the first day of the new year, many families visited the local temple to light incense and offer prayers. The temples were busiest at dawn, when visitors hoped to be the first to make their offerings -- a practice believed to bring exceptional good fortune. In 1998, temple courtyards filled up quickly with families in their new clothes.

👩

Visiting Relatives and Friends

Dressed in new clothes, families marched from one household to the next, bearing small gifts of fruit, candy, or homemade cakes. The host offered tea, passed around nuts, and exchanged a few words of blessing before sending everyone on their way. In 1998, a well-connected family could have three or four visits in a single day.

🙏

Ancestor Worship

Some families visited graves before the holiday, cleaning the stones and leaving offerings. Others held the ceremony at home, around a table with photographs and incense. Either way, the intention was the same: to say thank you. In 1998, the quiet act of remembrance felt more important than any public celebration.

For families celebrating in 1998, the Earth Tiger year carried a specific set of wishes: for health, for prosperity, for safety, and for the quiet happiness of a home in order.

👪

Family and Heritage

The festival renews family ties. Children see their grandparents, siblings travel long distances to sit at the same table, and relatives who have been out of touch exchange warm greetings. In 1998, this renewal was the deepest purpose of the celebration.

👪

Home and Hearth

Historical Context of 1998

The world in 1998 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Earth Tiger year began.

🌎

China in 1998

China in 1998 was riding the wave of economic growth that defined the 1990s. Deng Xiaoping's Southern Tour in 1992 had accelerated reforms, and the country was becoming a manufacturing powerhouse. Chinese New Year celebrations reflected this new prosperity, with more elaborate meals, bigger red envelopes, and increasingly commercial festivities.

🌐

Rising Global Power

The 1990s saw China's emergence as a major economic force. Hong Kong's return in 1997 was a defining moment of national pride. For Chinese New Year in 1998, the mood was one of confidence and ambition. The festival was no longer just a family occasion -- it was a showcase of Chinese culture to the world.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

By 1998, Chinese New Year had become a week-long national holiday. The Spring Festival travel rush was the largest annual human migration on Earth. Families traveled across the country to be together, and the festival's commercial aspects -- shopping, dining out, traveling -- were growing rapidly.

📚

Tradition as Stability

Despite the commercialization, the core of Chinese New Year remained unchanged in 1998. Families still gathered, ancestors were still honored, and the new year was still welcomed with hope. The festival proved that tradition and modernity could coexist.

A Story from 1998

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Earth Tiger year.

Meet Qi Feng, a factory worker born in a coastal town in Fujian. 1998 was supposed to be an ordinary Chinese New Year, but it turned into one of the holidays Qi would remember forever. It started with a small disaster: the stove in the family kitchen broke on New Year's Eve morning, right before the holiday meal was supposed to be cooked. The house felt suddenly, unexpectedly quiet. But neighbors rallied. One lent a portable stove; another sent over a large pot of already-steaming rice. A nearby shopkeeper kept his doors open a little longer just so the family could buy a replacement. By mid-afternoon, the kitchen was working again, if a little awkwardly, and by evening the dining table was full of dishes after all. The meal that year tasted better than usual, perhaps because it had nearly not happened. Qi learned something simple about the Earth Tiger year: that good fortune sometimes looks like ordinary people helping each other out. After midnight, as the firecrackers went off up and down the street, Qi wrote a short note in a notebook -- 'Remember the stove.'
