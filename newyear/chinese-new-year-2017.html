Spring Festival Customs in 2017

How did people actually celebrate Chinese New Year in 2017? The customs were deeply rooted in centuries of tradition, with regional variations that made each celebration unique. This year, families focused on six favorite rituals -- each one chosen for its special meaning and its place in the household's routine.

🎓

Reunion Dinner

The reunion dinner was where the year's stories were told. Grandparents recalled festivals from their youth, parents quietly counted the blessings of the past twelve months, and children asked for seconds of everything. In 2017, this meal was the emotional center of the holiday.

👩

Visiting Relatives and Friends

Dressed in new clothes, families marched from one household to the next, bearing small gifts of fruit, candy, or homemade cakes. The host offered tea, passed around nuts, and exchanged a few words of blessing before sending everyone on their way. In 2017, a well-connected family could have three or four visits in a single day.

🎁

Spring Couplets and Door Gods

Upstairs windows got smaller red decorations, too -- the inverted character for fortune, paper cuttings of the zodiac animal, and simple lucky phrases. The effect was a house that glowed red from the outside. In 2017, preparing all this paper took a full afternoon.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

Preparation started early. Houses were cleaned from top to bottom, debts were settled where possible, and new items were bought so that nothing worn or broken carried into the new year. Shopping in 2017 was a long, slow pleasure -- a chance to stroll, to bargain gently, and to plan every detail of the coming festival.

🎇

Firecrackers and Fireworks

Some families preferred long, winding strings of firecrackers hung from rooftops; others lit individual fireworks that sent colored sparks spiraling up into the dark sky. Both styles were equally beloved. In 2017, firecracker shops stayed open late, their shelves piled high with red paper tubes.

🧹

Spring Cleaning

Before the new year arrived, every house was cleaned from top to bottom. The act of sweeping away dust was symbolic -- it removed the old year's bad luck and made space for fresh fortune. In 2017, even the busiest households found time for a thorough cleaning in the last few days of December.

Historical Context of 2017

The world in 2017 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Fire Rooster year began.

🌎

China in 2017

China in 2017 was a global superpower with the world's second-largest economy. Technology was transforming daily life -- smartphones, mobile payments, and e-commerce were reshaping how people celebrated Chinese New Year. Digital red envelopes sent via WeChat became a new tradition alongside ancient customs.

🌐

The Digital Age

The 2010s brought unprecedented technological change to China. For Chinese New Year in 2017, this meant that families separated by distance could connect via video calls, red envelopes could be sent with a tap, and the Spring Festival Gala was streamed on multiple platforms. The festival was adapting to the digital era.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

Life in 2017 China was fast-paced and digitally connected. Yet during Chinese New Year, people still made the journey home. The Chunyun travel rush reached record numbers, with billions of trips made by rail, road, and air. The desire to be with family during the festival was stronger than ever.

📚

Tradition as Stability

Even as technology transformed every aspect of life, Chinese New Year in 2017 proved that some things don't change. The reunion dinner, the ancestor worship, the wish for good fortune -- these rituals connected the digital generation to their grandparents' generation.

A Story from 2017

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Fire Rooster year.

Meet Jia Tao, a fisherman in a quiet neighborhood in Shanghai. In 2017, Jia was preparing for Chinese New Year the way she always did -- by helping at home, running errands for the family, and quietly hoping the coming Fire Rooster year would bring something good. The week before the festival, the market was busier than usual. Jia came home with bags of rice, a string of firecrackers wrapped in brown paper, and a small red envelope the shopkeeper slipped to her with a wink. On New Year's Eve, the house was full. The stove stayed hot from morning until late at night, the siblings ran errands between the kitchen and the dining table, and the grandmother sat by the window folding paper envelopes while telling stories of festivals from her youth. At midnight, Jia lit the longest string of firecrackers the family could afford, and the whole neighborhood watched the sparks climb into the dark sky. Years later, Jia would remember 2017 not for anything dramatic, but for the feeling of the family gathered in one room -- steam from the hot dishes curling against the ceiling, red decorations glowing on every wall, and the quiet certainty that whatever came next, they would face it together.
