Spring Festival Customs in 1990

How did people actually celebrate Chinese New Year in 1990? The customs were deeply rooted in centuries of tradition, with regional variations that made each celebration unique. This year, families focused on six favorite rituals -- each one chosen for its special meaning and its place in the household's routine.

👗

Wearing New Clothes

New clothes for the new year were a firm tradition. A child who had worn the same jacket for months would be measured and taken to the tailor, or a bolt of cloth would be brought home so that a mother could sew something herself. In 1990, the first wearing of the new outfit happened on New Year's Day itself.

👜

New Year Shopping and Preparation

Special foods dominated the last week of preparation. Glutinous rice cakes were steamed, dried tofu was pressed, and candied fruits were jarred. Each item had its own symbolic value, from longevity to prosperity. In 1990, the kitchen was never busier than on the day before New Year's Eve.

🪐

Lion and Dragon Dances

A proper dragon dance needed dozens of men, each holding a pole that supported a section of the long, colorful body. As they moved in unison, the dragon seemed to swim through the street, coiling and uncoiling. In 1990, such performances were reserved for the biggest village fairs and city festivals.

🎁

Spring Couplets and Door Gods

Couplets were chosen with real thought. A family running a shop would pick phrases about prosperity; a household with schoolchildren would choose verses about study and wisdom. Many families in 1990 wrote their own, carefully practicing the characters until they felt just right.

🎓

Reunion Dinner

Long tables, short chairs, and steam rising above it all -- that was New Year's Eve. Every dish carried a wish: the fish represented having more than enough, the dumplings resembled ancient money, and the round tangyuan symbolized the family coming together. In 1990, the menu was planned weeks in advance.

🎇

Firecrackers and Fireworks

As midnight approached, the crackle of firecrackers rolled through every neighborhood. Children covered their ears and laughed; elders lit the longest strings they could afford. The smoke hung low over the rooftops, mixing with the smell of cooking oil and incense. In 1990, a village without fireworks on New Year's Eve would have felt unfinished.

Historical Context of 1990

The world in 1990 was a place of tension and transformation. Here's what was happening when the Metal Horse year began.

🌎

China in 1990

China in 1990 was riding the wave of economic growth that defined the 1990s. Deng Xiaoping's Southern Tour in 1992 had accelerated reforms, and the country was becoming a manufacturing powerhouse. Chinese New Year celebrations reflected this new prosperity, with more elaborate meals, bigger red envelopes, and increasingly commercial festivities.

🌐

Rising Global Power

The 1990s saw China's emergence as a major economic force. Hong Kong's return in 1997 was a defining moment of national pride. For Chinese New Year in 1990, the mood was one of confidence and ambition. The festival was no longer just a family occasion -- it was a showcase of Chinese culture to the world.

🏛

Daily Life and Resilience

By 1990, Chinese New Year had become a week-long national holiday. The Spring Festival travel rush was the largest annual human migration on Earth. Families traveled across the country to be together, and the festival's commercial aspects -- shopping, dining out, traveling -- were growing rapidly.

📚

Tradition as Stability

Despite the commercialization, the core of Chinese New Year remained unchanged in 1990. Families still gathered, ancestors were still honored, and the new year was still welcomed with hope. The festival proved that tradition and modernity could coexist.

A Story from 1990

The true meaning of Chinese New Year is often found not in grand history books, but in small human memories. Here is one such story from the Metal Horse year.

Meet Lan Ying, a factory worker in a riverside village in Zhejiang. 1990 started quietly. The weeks before Chinese New Year were colder than usual, and Lan spent most evenings at home helping a younger sibling practice writing auspicious characters on cheap red paper. The sibling was clumsy at first -- the ink ran, the characters were too large, the paper got wrinkled -- but by the third evening, the work started to look respectable. On New Year's Eve, when the time came to paste the couplets up, the family chose the sibling's best attempt rather than buying professionally written ones. The characters were a little wobbly, the ink was a little uneven, but the words were honest and meant something. Neighbors who walked past the door sometimes glanced twice at the writing, then smiled; they could tell it was a child's work, and they approved. Lan would later say that this small act -- letting a child write something important -- was what the Metal Horse year meant for the family. The festival, after all, was not about perfection. It was about passing something on.
