Spring Festival Customs in 1947

The 1947 festival kept the customs families returned to every year. Each one tied the household to a shared habit, from food to decoration to ceremony. These are six of the ones that mattered most in the Fire Pig year.

01

New Year Shopping

In the days before the festival, the market ran late. Stalls of red paper, dried fruit, fireworks, and cloth crowded the lanes. In 1947, shoppers carried bundles home through streets strung with lanterns.

02

Reunion Dinner

The night before the new year, families gathered around one table. Fish for surplus, dumplings for wealth, long noodles for long life, a chicken left whole to keep the family whole. In 1947, this was the meal people traveled all year to reach.

03

Red Envelopes

Married adults tucked crisp bills into red envelopes and pressed them into children's hands. The amount mattered less than the gesture. In 1947, a child might carry a stack of hongbao home and count them late into the night.

04

New Clothes

Red was the favorite, but any bright color would do. Children wore what made them happy; adults leaned toward darker, quieter tones. In 1947, a child in a new red coat walking to a relative's house was the picture of the holiday.

05

Lantern Night

On the fifteenth night, lanterns went up. Children carried rabbits and fish made of paper and wire, candlelit from inside. In 1947, the festival closed the way it opened, with light.

06

Spring Couplets

Red paper strips went up on every door, black ink brushed in pairs, with a shorter line across the top. Door gods stood guard at the entrance. In 1947, a good calligrapher could fill half a street with orders in the days before the festival.

Cultural Context of 1947

👪

Returning Relatives

After years apart, relatives found their way back to the family home. Some arrived thin, some arrived with new families, and the table grew to fit them all. The reunion dinner in 1947 was, for many, the first in a long time that felt complete.

🍲

Neighborhood Feasts

When one household could not manage a full dinner alone, neighbors pooled stoves and ingredients. A shared meal down the lane replaced the private one in the kitchen, and the cooking smell belonged to everyone.

👗

Mending and Making

New clothes were scarce, so old ones were taken in, let out, and dyed red where they could be. A child's festival outfit in 1947 was often three garments stitched into one.

🏯

Quiet Temple Visits

Temples opened their gates, and families came with incense and small offerings. The visits were shorter and quieter than before, but they restored a rhythm people had missed.

A Story from 1947

The meaning of the 1947 festival often lives less in records than in small memories. Here is one from the Fire Pig year.

Meet Hui Ying, a carpenter living in a fishing island in Fujian. 1947 was supposed to be an ordinary Chinese New Year, but it became one Hui Ying would not forget. It started with a small disaster: the kitchen stove broke on New Year's Eve morning, right when the reunion meal was meant to be cooked. The house went quiet. Then neighbors stepped in. One lent a portable stove, another sent a pot of steaming rice, and a shopkeeper kept her door open an extra hour so the family could buy a replacement. By late afternoon the kitchen worked again, and by evening the table was full. The meal that year tasted better, maybe because it almost did not happen. Hui Ying took a simple lesson from the Fire Pig year: good fortune often looks like neighbors looking out for each other. After midnight, with firecrackers rolling down the street, Hui Ying wrote a line in a notebook: remember the stove.

When was Chinese New Year in 1947?
