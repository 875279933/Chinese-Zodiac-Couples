1950 Lunar Calendar Explained

The Chinese lunar calendar and the Gregorian calendar don't follow the same rules. Here's how that affects the 1950 year of the Chinese zodiac.

The Year of Geng Yin

In the traditional Chinese lunar calendar, 1950 was the year of Geng Yin. "Geng" stands for Metal in the Heavenly Stems system, while "Yin" maps to the Tiger in the Earthly Branches. This particular pairing only rolls around once every 60 years.

Lunar Year Dates for 1950

The 1950 lunar year kicked off on Feb 17, 1950 and wrapped up on Feb 5, 1951. If your birthday falls in that window, you're a Metal Tiger. But if you were born earlier in Feb 1950 -- before the 17 -- you'd actually be the previous zodiac sign instead.

How the Lunar Calendar Works

The Chinese lunar calendar tracks the moon's phases, with each month starting on the new moon. A standard lunar year runs about 354 days across 12 months. To keep pace with the solar year, a leap month gets tacked on roughly every three years -- which is why Chinese New Year jumps around on the Western calendar.

Why Yang Metal Matters

The Geng Metal in the 1950 year of the Chinese zodiac is Yang Metal -- Yang energy is active, outward, and assertive. It pushes things forward and gives people born under its influence a natural confidence and drive. Metal adds resolve and discipline. People born in this year are tougher and more focused than others of their sign, with an inner strength that keeps them on track no matter what.

1950 in the Gregorian Calendar

What does 1950 look like through the lens of the Western calendar, and why does the date cutoff matter for the Chinese zodiac?

1950 on the Western Calendar

1950 on the Gregorian calendar is straightforward -- January 1 through December 31. But in Chinese culture, the year pulsed with the Metal Tiger's determined, resolute energy, shaping how people born that year see the world.

Why the Dates Don't Line Up

The 1950 year of the Chinese zodiac doesn't run from January 1 to December 31. Because Chinese New Year shifts each year, the zodiac year straddles two Gregorian years. For 1950, the Metal Tiger period ran from Feb 17, 1950 through Feb 5, 1951.

Check Your Birthdate Carefully

This matters more than most people realize. Born Feb 17 1950 or earlier? You're the previous sign, not a Tiger. The lunar calendar dates are the ones that count -- always double-check if your birthday falls near the Chinese New Year cutoff.

Famous People Born in 1950

1950 produced a wild mix of entertainers and creators. Here are six Earth Tigers you should know.

Stevie Wonder

Born May 13 in Saginaw, Michigan. Wonder signed with Motown at 11 and had his first #1 hit by 13. Songs in the Key of Life won Album of the Year at the Grammys in 1977, and he's won 25 Grammys total, the most ever for a solo artist.

Tom Petty

Born October 20 in Gainesville, Florida. Petty fronted Tom Petty and the Heartbreakers for 40 years, scored hits with "Free Fallin'," "Refugee," and "American Girl," and joined the Traveling Wilburys with Bob Dylan and George Harrison. He was inducted into the Rock Hall in 2002.

Jay Leno

Born April 28 in New Rochelle, New York. Leno hosted The Tonight Show for 22 seasons (1992-2009 and 2010-2014), the second-longest run in the show's history behind Johnny Carson. He still does around 200 stand-up shows a year.

Neil Young

Born November 12 in Toronto. Young co-founded Buffalo Springfield and then went solo, releasing "Heart of Gold," "Rockin' in the Free World," and the album Harvest (1972) which hit #1 in the U.S. He's been inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame twice.

Cybill Shepherd

Born February 18 in Memphis. Shepherd was on the cover of Glamour at 20, then landed the lead in The Last Picture Show (1971). She won two Golden Globes for Moonlighting alongside Bruce Willis in the 1980s.

Ed Harris

Born November 28 in Englewood, New Jersey. Harris has been nominated for four Academy Awards, most notably for Pollock (2000), which he also directed. Apollo 13, The Truman Show, and A Beautiful Mind are all in his filmography.
