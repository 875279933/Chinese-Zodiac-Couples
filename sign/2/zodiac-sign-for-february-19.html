Field Notes — A Tuesday in the Studio, February 19

Indira Banerjee was 32, a ceramicist in Providence, and the kind of artist who had a kiln in her garage and a cat who had opinions about the kiln. She had been making pots for 9 years. She had, in that time, sold enough of them to pay the rent on her studio, which was a small, concrete-floored room above a bakery. The bakery made excellent bread. The studio smelled, on a good day, like glaze and, on a better day, like glaze and bread.

On a Wednesday in mid-February, she went to the studio at 9, put on a podcast she had been meaning to listen to, and threw 14 mugs. Throwing mugs was, in her professional view, the right kind of project for a Wednesday. The first three were bad. The fourth was good. The fifth through eighth were, in her estimation, mixed. The last six were good. She had, in other words, gotten into a rhythm, which was, in her professional view, the only reason to do anything.

By 4 o'clock, the mugs were drying, the studio was a small mess of clay, and she was eating a slice of bread from the bakery downstairs, which the baker had brought up at 2 without being asked. She drank a cup of tea, looked at the mugs, and thought, as she often did on Wednesdays, that this was a good job. It was not a famous job. It was not a rich job. It was, in fact, a job that paid her rent and gave her 14 mugs and a slice of bread on a Wednesday in February. That was, in her view, the right kind of job.
