Field Notes — The Park Bench, February 20

Sam Goldberg was 58, a high school English teacher in Pittsburgh, and the kind of man who had been teaching To Kill a Mockingbird to 10th graders for 23 years. He had read it 23 times, which was, in his professional view, both a privilege and a thing he never quite got used to. He had a wife, two grown sons, a dog, and a small house with a porch that was, in summer, his favorite place. It was February. The porch was, by his own admission, less his favorite place in February.

On a Saturday in mid-February, he went to the park with the dog. The dog, Penny, was 9 and had slowed down in the way 9-year-old dogs do, which is to say she was still interested in the park but had started to take the long way around. Sam had started to do the same. He sat on a bench. Penny sniffed a bush. The sky was the kind of grey that promises nothing in particular, which was, in his view, the right kind of sky for a Saturday in February.

He thought about a student he had taught 10 years ago who had just emailed him, which is the kind of email a teacher gets maybe twice a decade. He thought about the book. He thought about a sentence he had written in a margin that he had not been able to read since. He thought, briefly, about retirement, which was, in his view, both closer and further than it sounded. Penny came back. They walked the long way around. They went home. The day, in other words, was the right size. The sky was the right sky. The dog was the right dog.
