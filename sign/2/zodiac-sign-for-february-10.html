A Reader Story — A Small Repair, February 10

Joaquin Reyes was 44, a furniture maker in Asheville, and a man who had recently inherited his father's workbench. The workbench was older than him, older than the marriage that had produced him, and the wood still smelled like the shop it had come from. He used it most days. He thought about his father on most days, in the way sons think about their fathers, which is to say sometimes clearly and sometimes not at all.

On a Wednesday in early February, he got up early, made coffee, and went out to the shop. The day was a maintenance day: a chair that needed a re-glue, a drawer slide that had been sticking, a small piece of walnut he had been saving for the right project. The chair, when he got to it, was a 30-minute job. The drawer was, in the end, a 90-minute job. The walnut, he decided, was for something he had not yet designed, which was fine.

By the afternoon, the shop was a small mess of shavings and the kind of quiet that comes with repetitive handwork. He swept up, ate a sandwich on the workbench, and thought about a piece he had been meaning to design for a client. He thought about his father. He thought about the chair, which was now solid in a way it had not been for a year. By 4 o'clock he was back in the house, washing his hands, listening to a record his wife had left on, and considering, with no particular urgency, what to make for dinner. The walnut would wait. It was good at waiting.
