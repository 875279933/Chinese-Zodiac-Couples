Field Notes — Sunday in the Kitchen, February 21

Camille Rousseau was 45, a pastry chef in Montreal, and the kind of person who could make a croquembouche in her sleep, which she had, in fact, done once. She had a small shop in the Plateau, a husband who was an architect, a daughter in college, and a recipe book she had been writing in pencil for 18 years. The book was not, in her view, a book. It was a series of notes. It was, however, getting closer to being a book.

On a Sunday in late February, she made croissants. This was, in her professional view, the right kind of project for a Sunday. The dough had to rest overnight. The folding was a slow, careful process. The butter had to be the right temperature, which was, in her professional view, a temperature that took 12 years to learn. She made 24. They were, by her professional opinion, mostly good. Two were, by her professional opinion, very good.

By 4 o'clock, the croissants were on a cooling rack, the kitchen was a small mess of flour, and her husband had come in from a walk and eaten one, standing, without asking. He said it was good. He did not say it was the best. She did not need him to say it was the best. He had been eating her croissants for 20 years and he had, in that time, learned when a croissant was good. The day was, in other words, the right size. The book was, in fact, getting closer to being a book.
