Field Notes — A Window Seat, February 18

Hana Okonkwo was 35, a violinist in the Cincinnati Symphony, and the kind of person who had, over 12 years, learned to read a conductor's bad moods from the third row. She had a small apartment in a good neighborhood, a boyfriend who was a sound engineer, and a cat named Brahms, which was a joke her father had made when she was 9 and which had, somehow, stuck. She played in the second violin section. She was, by her own account, very good.

On a Tuesday in mid-February, she had a day off, which was rare. She slept until 9, made coffee, and read the paper on the window seat. The window seat was a small project she had done with her father when she was 23, and which was, in her view, the best piece of furniture she had ever owned. It fit her exactly. She read for an hour.

By the afternoon, she had gone for a walk, run a small errand, and made a stew that was the same stew she always made, with carrots cut the way she always cut them. Her boyfriend came home at 6, and they ate at the kitchen counter, with Brahms sitting on the kitchen table, which was technically not allowed. They talked about a concert coming up. They talked about a friend having a baby. They talked, briefly, about getting a dog, which was a conversation they had been having for 11 months and which was, in her view, going to keep happening. By 9, the stew was in the fridge. By 10, she was in the window seat with a novel. The day, in other words, was the right size.
