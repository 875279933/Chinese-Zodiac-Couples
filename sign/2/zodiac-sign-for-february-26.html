Reader Portrait — A Tuesday in March Practice, February 26

Theo Mensah was 17, a high school junior in Cleveland, and the kind of teenager who had recently discovered that he was, in fact, fairly good at chess. He had been playing for a year. He had, in that time, gone from 800 to 1600 on the online rating, which is a jump, which is the kind of jump his coach had said was not, in fact, normal. He had a small chess board in his bedroom, a notebook he was using to record his games, and a vague idea that he might try to play in college, which is, at 17, both a big idea and a small one.

On a Tuesday in late February, he had his regular practice with his coach, a 60-year-old retired postal worker named Sergei, who had, in Theo's opinion, the right idea about most things in chess. They played two games. Theo lost the first, won the second. Sergei said, after the second, that Theo's opening was getting better. Theo did not, in fact, know his opening was getting better. He had, however, been working on it.

By the time he got home, it was dark. His mother was making dinner. His father was in the kitchen, talking to her about a faucet. He sat at the table and thought, briefly, about the first game he had lost, which was, in his view, a small mistake on move 22. He thought, briefly, about the second game, which was, in his view, less small. He ate the dinner, which was rice and chicken, which his mother had made, which was, in his view, the right kind of dinner for a Tuesday. By 9, he was in bed. By 10, he was asleep. The year, in his view, was going to be fine.
